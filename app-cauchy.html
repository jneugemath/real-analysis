This appendix gives a second proof of Theorem 1.7.2, due to Georg Cantor. It builds \(\mathbb{R}\) out of Cauchy sequences of rational numbers, as in Section 3.5.

In \(\mathbb{Q}\), a Cauchy sequence need not converge. A sequence of rational numbers can close in on \(\sqrt2\), with its terms getting closer and closer together, and still have no rational limit (Exercise B.1.1). Cantor’s idea was to let such a sequence stand for the number it ought to converge to. Two Cauchy sequences whose difference tends to \(0\) should stand for the same number. So a real number will be a whole class of Cauchy sequences, all of them closing in on the same point.

As in Appendix A, only \(\mathbb{Q}\) is assumed, with its usual operations and order. The results of Sections 1.4, 1.5 and 1.6 hold in \(\mathbb{Q}\), and nothing from Section 1.7 or later is used. In particular, every \(\varepsilon\) in this appendix is a positive rational number. Appendix A is not needed, except for Lemma A.1.5, the Archimedean Principle for \(\mathbb{Q}\). In this appendix, \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\) denote sequences \(\{x_n\}\), \(\{y_n\}\) and \(\{z_n\}\) of rational numbers, and \(p\), \(q\), \(r\) and \(c\) denote rational numbers.

B.1 Cauchy Sequences of Rational Numbers

Definition B.1.1. A sequence \(x=\{x_n\}\) of rational numbers is a Cauchy sequence in \(\mathbb{Q}\) if for every rational \(\varepsilon>0\), there exists an \(N\in\mathbb{N}\) such that \(|x_m-x_n|<\varepsilon\) for all \(m,n\ge N\). It is a null sequence if for every rational \(\varepsilon>0\), there exists an \(N\in\mathbb{N}\) such that \(|x_n|<\varepsilon\) for all \(n\ge N\). Let \(\mathcal{C}\) denote the set of all Cauchy sequences in \(\mathbb{Q}\).

Sums, differences and products of sequences are taken term by term. So \(x+y\) is the sequence \(\{x_n+y_n\}\), and \(xy\) is \(\{x_ny_n\}\). A rational number \(q\) also stands for the constant sequence \(q,q,q,\dots\). Every constant sequence is Cauchy. By Lemma A.1.5, the sequence \(\left\{\frac1n\right\}\) is a null sequence. Given \(\varepsilon>0\), choose \(N\in\mathbb{N}\) with \(N\varepsilon>1\). Then \(\left|\frac1n\right|\le\frac1N<\varepsilon\) for \(n\ge N\).

Lemma B.1.2. Let \(x,y\in\mathcal{C}\), and let \(z\) and \(w\) be null sequences.

  1. There is a rational \(M>0\) with \(|x_n|\le M\) for all \(n\in\mathbb{N}\).

  2. The sequences \(x+y\), \(x-y\) and \(xy\) are in \(\mathcal{C}\).

  3. The sequences \(z\), \(z+w\) and \(xz\) are null sequences, and \(z\in\mathcal{C}\).

Proof.

(1) Choose \(N\) with \(|x_m-x_n|<1\) for all \(m,n\ge N\). Then \(|x_n|<1+|x_N|\) for \(n\ge N\). Let \(M=\max\{|x_1|,\dots,|x_{N-1}|,1+|x_N|\}\), as in the proof of Theorem 3.5.2.

(2) Let \(\varepsilon>0\). Choose \(M\) as in (1) for both \(x\) and \(y\). Choose \(N\) with \(|x_m-x_n|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2M}\) and \(|y_m-y_n|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2M}\) for all \(m,n\ge N\). Then for \(m,n\ge N\), \[|x_my_m-x_ny_n|=|x_m(y_m-y_n)+y_n(x_m-x_n)|\le M|y_m-y_n|+M|x_m-x_n|<\varepsilon.\] So \(xy\in\mathcal{C}\). The proofs for \(x+y\) and \(x-y\) are the same, with \(\frac\varepsilon 2\) in place of \(\frac\varepsilon{2M}\) and \(|(x_m\pm y_m)-(x_n\pm y_n)|\le|x_m-x_n|+|y_m-y_n|\).

(3) Let \(\varepsilon>0\). Choose \(N\) with \(|z_n|<\frac\varepsilon 2\) and \(|w_n|<\frac\varepsilon 2\) for \(n\ge N\). Then \(|z_n+w_n|<\varepsilon\) and \(|z_m-z_n|\le|z_m|+|z_n|<\varepsilon\) for \(m,n\ge N\). So \(z+w\) is null and \(z\in\mathcal{C}\). With \(M\) as in (1), choose \(N\) with \(|z_n|<\frac\varepsilon M\) for \(n\ge N\). Then \(|x_nz_n|\le M|z_n|<\varepsilon\). So \(xz\) is null.

∎

Definition B.1.3. For \(x,y\in\mathcal{C}\), write \(x\sim y\) if \(x-y\) is a null sequence.

Lemma B.1.4. The relation \(\sim\) is an equivalence relation on \(\mathcal{C}\).

Proof.

Since \(x-x\) is the constant sequence \(0\), \(x\sim x\). If \(x\sim y\), then \(y\sim x\), since \(|y_n-x_n|=|x_n-y_n|\). If \(x\sim y\) and \(y\sim z\), then \(x-z=(x-y)+(y-z)\) is null by Lemma B.1.2 (3). So \(x\sim z\).

∎

Definition B.1.5. For \(x\in\mathcal{C}\), let \([x]=\{y\in\mathcal{C}:y\sim x\}\) be its equivalence class. In this appendix, \(\mathbb{R}\) denotes the set of all equivalence classes \([x]\) with \(x\in\mathcal{C}\). For \(q\in\mathbb{Q}\), \([q]\) denotes the class of the constant sequence \(q,q,q,\dots\).

So \([x]=[y]\) exactly when \(x\sim y\), and \([x]=[0]\) exactly when \(x\) is a null sequence. For example, \(\left[\left\{\frac1n\right\}\right]=[0]\) and \(\left[\left\{1+\frac1n\right\}\right]=[1]\).

B.2 Addition and Multiplication

Definition B.2.1. For \([x],[y]\in\mathbb{R}\), define \([x]+[y]=[x+y]\) and \([x][y]=[xy]\).

Lemma B.2.2. These operations are well defined. That is, if \(x\sim x'\) and \(y\sim y'\), then \(x+y\sim x'+y'\) and \(xy\sim x'y'\).

Proof.

By Lemma B.1.2 (2), all four sequences are in \(\mathcal{C}\). Now \((x+y)-(x'+y')=(x-x')+(y-y')\) is a sum of two null sequences, so it is null. Also \[xy-x'y'=x(y-y')+y'(x-x').\] Each term is a Cauchy sequence times a null sequence, so it is null by Lemma B.1.2 (3). So their sum is null.

∎

Proof Technique (Checking That an Operation Is Well Defined).

A real number in this appendix is a class with many representatives. The sum \([x]+[y]\) is computed from one representative of each class. If another choice of representatives gave a different class, the sum would not make sense. So every definition made through representatives needs a check that the answer does not depend on the choice. Lemma B.2.2 is that check for sums and products, and Lemma B.3.2 (1) is that check for the order. The same issue comes up whenever objects are defined as equivalence classes, as in the definition of \(\mathbb{Q}\) itself, where \(\frac12=\frac24\).

Lemma B.2.3. Let \(x\in\mathcal{C}\) be a sequence that is not null. Then there are a rational \(c>0\) and an \(N\in\mathbb{N}\) such that either \(x_n\ge c\) for all \(n\ge N\), or \(x_n\le-c\) for all \(n\ge N\).

Proof.

Since \(x\) is not null, there is a rational \(\varepsilon>0\) such that for every \(N\), some \(n\ge N\) has \(|x_n|\ge\varepsilon\). Let \(c=\frac\varepsilon 2\). Choose \(N\) with \(|x_m-x_n|<c\) for all \(m,n\ge N\), and then choose \(k\ge N\) with \(|x_k|\ge2c\). If \(x_k\ge2c\), then \(x_n>x_k-c\ge c\) for all \(n\ge N\). If \(x_k\le-2c\), then \(x_n<x_k+c\le-c\) for all \(n\ge N\).

∎

Theorem B.2.4. With the operations of Definition B.2.1, \(\mathbb{R}\) is a field. Its zero is \([0]\) and its one is \([1]\).

Proof.

Axioms (1) and (6) of Definition 1.5.1 hold by Lemmas B.1.2 and B.2.2. Axioms (2), (3), (7), (8) and (11) hold because they hold term by term in \(\mathbb{Q}\). For example, \[[x]\bigl([y]+[z]\bigr)=[x(y+z)]=[xy+xz]=[x][y]+[x][z],\] since \(x_n(y_n+z_n)=x_ny_n+x_nz_n\) for every \(n\). For (4) and (5), \([0]+[x]=[x]\) and \([x]+[-x]=[0]\), where \(-x=\{-x_n\}\). For (9), \([1][x]=[x]\), and \([1]\ne[0]\), since the constant sequence \(1\) is not null.

For (10), let \([x]\ne[0]\). Then \(x\) is not null. By Lemma B.2.3, there are a rational \(c>0\) and an \(N\) with \(|x_n|\ge c\) for all \(n\ge N\). Define \(y_n=\frac1{x_n}\) for \(n\ge N\) and \(y_n=0\) for \(n<N\). For \(m,n\ge N\), \[|y_m-y_n|=\frac{|x_n-x_m|}{|x_m||x_n|}\le\frac{|x_m-x_n|}{c^2}.\] Given \(\varepsilon>0\), choose \(N'\ge N\) with \(|x_m-x_n|<c^2\varepsilon\) for all \(m,n\ge N'\). Then \(|y_m-y_n|<\varepsilon\) for \(m,n\ge N'\). So \(y\in\mathcal{C}\). Also \(x_ny_n=1\) for all \(n\ge N\), so \(xy-1\) is null, and \([x][y]=[1]\).

∎

B.3 Order

Definition B.3.1. A sequence \(x\in\mathcal{C}\) is positive if there are a rational \(c>0\) and an \(N\in\mathbb{N}\) with \(x_n\ge c\) for all \(n\ge N\). For \([x],[y]\in\mathbb{R}\), define \([x]<[y]\) to mean that \(y-x\) is positive.

Lemma B.3.2. Let \(x,y\in\mathcal{C}\).

  1. If \(x\) is positive and \(y\sim x\), then \(y\) is positive.

  2. Exactly one of the following holds. The sequence \(x\) is positive, \(x\) is null, or \(-x\) is positive.

  3. If \(x\) and \(y\) are positive, so are \(x+y\) and \(xy\).

Proof.

(1) Let \(x_n\ge c\) for \(n\ge N\). Choose \(N'\ge N\) with \(|y_n-x_n|<\frac c2\) for \(n\ge N'\). Then \(y_n>x_n-\frac c2\ge\frac c2\) for \(n\ge N'\).

(2) If \(x\) is null, then \(|x_n|<c\) for large \(n\), for every \(c>0\). So neither \(x\) nor \(-x\) is positive. If \(x\) and \(-x\) were both positive, then \(x_n\ge c\) and \(-x_n\ge c'\) for large \(n\), which is impossible. So at most one of the three holds. If \(x\) is not null, Lemma B.2.3 shows that \(x\) or \(-x\) is positive.

(3) If \(x_n\ge c\) for \(n\ge N\) and \(y_n\ge c'\) for \(n\ge N'\), then for \(n\ge\max\{N,N'\}\), \(x_n+y_n\ge c+c'\) and \(x_ny_n\ge cc'\).

∎

Theorem B.3.3. With the order of Definition B.3.1, \(\mathbb{R}\) is an ordered field. An element \([x]\) is positive in the sense of Definition 1.5.6 if and only if the sequence \(x\) is positive.

Proof.

If \(x\sim x'\) and \(y\sim y'\), then \(y-x\sim y'-x'\). So by Lemma B.3.2 (1), whether \(y-x\) is positive does not depend on the choice of \(x\) and \(y\) in their classes. So \(<\) is well defined.

Next, \(<\) satisfies trichotomy. To see this, let \(a=[x]\) and \(b=[y]\). By Lemma B.3.2 (2), exactly one of the following holds. The sequence \(y-x\) is positive, \(y-x\) is null, or \(x-y\) is positive. These say \(a<b\), \(a=b\) and \(b<a\).

The relation is also transitive. If \([x]<[y]\) and \([y]<[z]\), then \(z-x=(z-y)+(y-x)\) is positive by Lemma B.3.2 (3). So \([x]<[z]\).

It remains to check the two properties in Definition 1.5.6. Property (1) holds since \((y+z)-(x+z)=y-x\). So \([x]\le[y]\) implies \([x]+[z]\le[y]+[z]\).

For property (2), let \([0]\le[x]\) and \([0]\le[y]\). If either is \([0]\), then \([x][y]=[0]\). Otherwise \(x\) and \(y\) are positive, so \(xy\) is positive by Lemma B.3.2 (3), and \([0]<[x][y]\).

Finally, \([0]<[x]\) means \(x-0=x\) is positive.

∎

Lemma B.3.4. Let \(x,y\in\mathcal{C}\). If there is an \(N\) with \(x_n\le y_n\) for all \(n\ge N\), then \([x]\le[y]\).

Proof.

Suppose \([y]<[x]\). Then \(x-y\) is positive, so there are \(c>0\) and \(N'\) with \(x_n-y_n\ge c\) for \(n\ge N'\). For \(n\ge\max\{N,N'\}\), this contradicts \(x_n\le y_n\). So \([x]\le[y]\).

∎

Theorem B.3.5. For all \(p,q\in\mathbb{Q}\), \([p+q]=[p]+[q]\) and \([pq]=[p][q]\), and \(p<q\) if and only if \([p]<[q]\). So the map \(q\mapsto[q]\) is one-to-one, and \(\{[q]:q\in\mathbb{Q}\}\) is a subfield of \(\mathbb{R}\).

Proof.

The first two statements hold by Definition B.2.1, since the operations on constant sequences are the operations of \(\mathbb{Q}\). The class \([q]-[p]=[q-p]\) is positive exactly when the constant sequence \(q-p\) is at least some \(c>0\), that is, when \(q-p>0\). So \(p<q\) if and only if \([p]<[q]\). In particular, \(p\ne q\) implies \([p]\ne[q]\). Also \([q]^{-1}=\bigl[\frac1q\bigr]\) for \(q\ne0\). So \(\{[q]:q\in\mathbb{Q}\}\) contains \([0]\) and \([1]\) and is closed under sums, products, negatives and reciprocals. As in the proof of Theorem A.5.2, it is a subfield of \(\mathbb{R}\).

∎

As in Section A.5, identify each \(q\in\mathbb{Q}\) with \([q]\). Then \(\mathbb{Q}\) is a subfield of \(\mathbb{R}\). In particular, if \(x\in\mathcal{C}\), then each term \(x_n\) is also a real number, namely \([x_n]\).

B.4 Completeness

The field \(\mathbb{R}\) is now an ordered field containing \(\mathbb{Q}\). It remains to prove the least upper bound property. The proof uses bisection, as in the proof of the Nested Interval Theorem (Theorem 3.4.4). The endpoints are rational numbers, so the sequences of endpoints are Cauchy sequences in \(\mathbb{Q}\), and they define a real number. Two lemmas come first. The first says \(\mathbb{Q}\) is spread throughout \(\mathbb{R}\). The second says a Cauchy sequence converges to its own class.

Lemma B.4.1.

  1. For every \(a\in\mathbb{R}\), there is an \(n\in\mathbb{N}\) with \(a<n\).

  2. For all \(a,b\in\mathbb{R}\) with \(a<b\), there is a \(q\in\mathbb{Q}\) with \(a<q<b\).

Proof.

(1) Let \(a=[x]\). By Lemma B.1.2 (1), \(x_n\le M\) for all \(n\), for some rational \(M\). By Lemma B.3.4, \(a\le M\). By Lemma A.1.5, \(M<n\) for some \(n\in\mathbb{N}\). So \(a<n\).

(2) Let \(a=[x]\) and \(b=[y]\). Since \(y-x\) is positive, there are \(c>0\) and \(N_1\) with \(y_n-x_n\ge c\) for all \(n\ge N_1\). Choose \(N\ge N_1\) with \(|x_m-x_n|<\frac c4\) and \(|y_m-y_n|<\frac c4\) for all \(m,n\ge N\). Let \(q=\frac12(x_N+y_N)\). For \(n\ge N\), \[q-x_n=\frac12(y_N-x_N)+(x_N-x_n)>\frac c2-\frac c4=\frac c4,\] and in the same way \(y_n-q=\frac12(y_N-x_N)+(y_n-y_N)>\frac c4\). So the sequences \(q-x\) and \(y-q\) are positive. That is, \(a<q\) and \(q<b\).

∎

Lemma B.4.2. Let \(x\in\mathcal{C}\) and \(a=[x]\). For every rational \(\varepsilon>0\), there is an \(N\in\mathbb{N}\) such that \(|a-x_n|\le\varepsilon\) for all \(n\ge N\).

Proof.

Choose \(N\) with \(|x_m-x_n|<\varepsilon\) for all \(m,n\ge N\). Fix \(n\ge N\). The class of the sequence \(\{x_m-x_n\}_{m=1}^\infty\) is \(a-x_n\). For \(m\ge N\), \(-\varepsilon<x_m-x_n<\varepsilon\). By Lemma B.3.4, \(-\varepsilon\le a-x_n\le\varepsilon\). So \(|a-x_n|\le\varepsilon\) by Theorem 1.6.2 (4).

∎

Theorem B.4.3. The ordered field \(\mathbb{R}\) has the least upper bound property.

Proof.

Let \(E\subset\mathbb{R}\) be nonempty and bounded above, say by \(B\). Choose \(e_0\in E\). By Lemma B.4.1 (1), there are \(j,k\in\mathbb{N}\) with \(B<k\) and \(-e_0<j\). Let \(a_0=-j\) and \(b_0=k\). Then \(a_0<e_0\le B<b_0\). So \(a_0\) is not an upper bound of \(E\), and \(b_0\) is an upper bound of \(E\).

The proof finds \(\sup E\) by bisection. Suppose rational numbers \(a_n<b_n\) have been chosen, where \(a_n\) is not an upper bound of \(E\) and \(b_n\) is. Let \(m=\frac12(a_n+b_n)\). If \(m\) is an upper bound of \(E\), let \(a_{n+1}=a_n\) and \(b_{n+1}=m\). Otherwise let \(a_{n+1}=m\) and \(b_{n+1}=b_n\). By induction, for all \(n\in\mathbb{N}_0\), \(a_n\) is not an upper bound of \(E\), \(b_n\) is an upper bound of \(E\), \[a_n\le a_{n+1}<b_{n+1}\le b_n,\quad\text{and}\quad b_n-a_n=\frac{b_0-a_0}{2^n}.\]

Next, the endpoints form Cauchy sequences in \(\mathbb{Q}\). To see this, let \(d=b_0-a_0\). If \(m\ge n\ge1\), then \(a_n\le a_m<b_m\le b_n\). So \(|a_m-a_n|\) and \(|b_m-b_n|\) are at most \(\frac d{2^n}<\frac dn\), using \(2^n>n\) (Exercise 1.2.9). Given \(\varepsilon>0\), choose \(N\in\mathbb{N}\) with \(N\varepsilon>d\) by Lemma A.1.5. Then \(|a_m-a_n|<\varepsilon\) and \(|b_m-b_n|<\varepsilon\) for all \(m,n\ge N\). Also \(b_n-a_n<\frac dn\), so \(\{b_n-a_n\}\) is null. Thus the sequences \(a=\{a_n\}\) and \(b=\{b_n\}\) are in \(\mathcal{C}\), and \(a\sim b\). Let \(s=[a]=[b]\).

The class \(s\) is the candidate for \(\sup E\). First, \(s\) is an upper bound of \(E\). To see this, let \(e\in E\), and suppose \(e>s\). By Lemma B.4.1 (2), there is a rational \(\varepsilon\) with \(0<\varepsilon<e-s\). By Lemma B.4.2, there is an \(n\) with \(|s-b_n|\le\varepsilon\). Then \(b_n\le s+\varepsilon<e\). This contradicts the fact that \(b_n\) is an upper bound of \(E\). So \(e\le s\).

Finally, no smaller number is an upper bound of \(E\). To see this, let \(t<s\). Choose a rational \(\varepsilon\) with \(0<\varepsilon<s-t\), and an \(n\) with \(|s-a_n|\le\varepsilon\). Then \(a_n\ge s-\varepsilon>t\). Since \(a_n\) is not an upper bound of \(E\), some \(e\in E\) has \(e>a_n>t\). So \(t\) is not an upper bound of \(E\). Therefore \(s=\sup E\).

∎

Second proof of Theorem 1.7.2.

By Theorem B.3.3, \(\mathbb{R}\) is an ordered field. By Theorem B.4.3, it has the least upper bound property. By Theorem B.3.5, after each \(q\in\mathbb{Q}\) is identified with \([q]\), \(\mathbb{Q}\) is a subfield of \(\mathbb{R}\).

∎

B.5 Uniqueness of the Real Numbers*

This section can be skipped. Nothing else depends on it.

Appendices A and B build two different objects. A real number is a set of rational numbers in one, and a class of sequences of rational numbers in the other. This section shows that the difference does not matter. Any two ordered fields with the least upper bound property are copies of each other. Every sum, product and inequality in one matches a sum, product and inequality in the other. So Theorem 1.7.2 describes \(\mathbb{R}\) completely, and it makes sense to speak of the real numbers.

First, every ordered field contains a copy of \(\mathbb{Q}\). For an ordered field \(\mathbb{F}\) and \(n\in\mathbb{N}\), let \(n_\mathbb{F}=1+1+\cdots+1\), with \(n\) terms. That is, \(1_\mathbb{F}=1\) and \((n+1)_\mathbb{F}=n_\mathbb{F}+1\). Let \(0_\mathbb{F}=0\) and \((-n)_\mathbb{F}=-n_\mathbb{F}\). This defines \(m_\mathbb{F}\) for every \(m\in\mathbb{Z}\).

Lemma B.5.1. Let \(\mathbb{F}\) be an ordered field. For \(m\in\mathbb{Z}\) and \(n\in\mathbb{N}\), define \(\iota_\mathbb{F}\bigl(\frac mn\bigr)=m_\mathbb{F}\,n_\mathbb{F}^{-1}\). Then \(\iota_\mathbb{F}:\mathbb{Q}\to\mathbb{F}\) is well defined, and for all \(p,q\in\mathbb{Q}\), \[\iota_\mathbb{F}(p+q)=\iota_\mathbb{F}(p)+\iota_\mathbb{F}(q),\quad\iota_\mathbb{F}(pq)=\iota_\mathbb{F}(p)\,\iota_\mathbb{F}(q),\quad\text{and}\quad p<q\iff\iota_\mathbb{F}(p)<\iota_\mathbb{F}(q).\]

Proof.

For all \(m,k\in\mathbb{Z}\), \((m+k)_\mathbb{F}=m_\mathbb{F}+k_\mathbb{F}\) and \((mk)_\mathbb{F}=m_\mathbb{F}\,k_\mathbb{F}\), and \(m<k\) implies \(m_\mathbb{F}<k_\mathbb{F}\). These are proved by induction, in Exercise B.5.1. In particular, \(n_\mathbb{F}>0\) for \(n\in\mathbb{N}\), so \(n_\mathbb{F}^{-1}\) exists.

If \(\frac mn=\frac jk\) with \(n,k\in\mathbb{N}\), then \(mk=nj\). So \(m_\mathbb{F}\,k_\mathbb{F}=n_\mathbb{F}\,j_\mathbb{F}\), and dividing by \(n_\mathbb{F}\,k_\mathbb{F}\) gives \(m_\mathbb{F}\,n_\mathbb{F}^{-1}=j_\mathbb{F}\,k_\mathbb{F}^{-1}\). So \(\iota_\mathbb{F}\) is well defined. For sums, \[\iota_\mathbb{F}\Bigl(\frac mn+\frac jk\Bigr)=\iota_\mathbb{F}\Bigl(\frac{mk+nj}{nk}\Bigr)=\frac{m_\mathbb{F}\,k_\mathbb{F}+n_\mathbb{F}\,j_\mathbb{F}}{n_\mathbb{F}\,k_\mathbb{F}}=\frac{m_\mathbb{F}}{n_\mathbb{F}}+\frac{j_\mathbb{F}}{k_\mathbb{F}}.\] Products are similar. For the order, \(\frac mn<\frac jk\) if and only if \(mk<nj\), which holds if and only if \(m_\mathbb{F}\,k_\mathbb{F}<n_\mathbb{F}\,j_\mathbb{F}\). Since \(n_\mathbb{F}\,k_\mathbb{F}>0\), this holds if and only if \(\frac{m_\mathbb{F}}{n_\mathbb{F}}<\frac{j_\mathbb{F}}{k_\mathbb{F}}\), by Theorem 1.5.8 (3) and (6).

∎

If \(\mathbb{F}\) also has the least upper bound property, then the proofs of the Archimedean Principle (Theorem 1.7.8), of Theorem 1.7.9, and of the density of the rational numbers (Theorem 1.7.11) work in \(\mathbb{F}\) word for word. They use only the axioms of an ordered field, the least upper bound property and the Well-Ordering Principle, with each integer \(n\) read as \(n_\mathbb{F}\). So for all \(x,y\in\mathbb{F}\) with \(x<y\), there is a \(q\in\mathbb{Q}\) with \(x<\iota_\mathbb{F}(q)<y\). It follows that two elements \(x\) and \(y\) of \(\mathbb{F}\) are equal if and only if \[\{q\in\mathbb{Q}:\iota_\mathbb{F}(q)<x\}=\{q\in\mathbb{Q}:\iota_\mathbb{F}(q)<y\}.\] Indeed, if \(x<y\), a rational number between them belongs to the second set and not the first. So an element of \(\mathbb{F}\) is determined by the rational numbers below it.

Theorem B.5.2. Let \(\mathbb{F}\) and \(\mathbb{G}\) be ordered fields with the least upper bound property. Then there is a one-to-one function \(\varphi\) from \(\mathbb{F}\) onto \(\mathbb{G}\) such that for all \(x,y\in\mathbb{F}\), \[\varphi(x+y)=\varphi(x)+\varphi(y),\quad\varphi(xy)=\varphi(x)\varphi(y),\quad\text{and}\quad x<y\iff\varphi(x)<\varphi(y).\] Also \(\varphi(\iota_\mathbb{F}(q))=\iota_{\mathbb{G}}(q)\) for every \(q\in\mathbb{Q}\).

Proof.

Write \(\iota=\iota_\mathbb{F}\) and \(\kappa=\iota_{\mathbb{G}}\). For \(x\in\mathbb{F}\), let \(C(x)=\{q\in\mathbb{Q}:\iota(q)<x\}\). By the Archimedean Principle in \(\mathbb{F}\), there are \(j,k\in\mathbb{N}\) with \(-j_\mathbb{F}<x<k_\mathbb{F}\). So \(-j\in C(x)\), and every \(q\in C(x)\) has \(q<k\), so \(\kappa(q)<k_{\mathbb{G}}\). Define \[\varphi(x)=\sup\{\kappa(q):q\in C(x)\},\] which exists in \(\mathbb{G}\) by the least upper bound property.

The proof has six steps. The first step shows that for \(q\in\mathbb{Q}\), \(\kappa(q)<\varphi(x)\) if and only if \(\iota(q)<x\). Suppose \(\iota(q)<x\). By density in \(\mathbb{F}\), there is an \(r\in\mathbb{Q}\) with \(\iota(q)<\iota(r)<x\). Then \(r\in C(x)\) and \(q<r\), so \(\kappa(q)<\kappa(r)\le\varphi(x)\). Conversely, suppose \(\kappa(q)<\varphi(x)\). Then \(\kappa(q)\) is not an upper bound of \(\{\kappa(r):r\in C(x)\}\). So \(\kappa(q)<\kappa(r)\) for some \(r\in C(x)\). Then \(q<r\), and \(\iota(q)<\iota(r)<x\).

The second step shows that \(\varphi\) sends each rational number of \(\mathbb{F}\) to the matching rational number of \(\mathbb{G}\). For \(p,q\in\mathbb{Q}\), the first step gives \(\kappa(q)<\varphi(\iota(p))\) if and only if \(\iota(q)<\iota(p)\), that is, \(q<p\), that is, \(\kappa(q)<\kappa(p)\). So \(\varphi(\iota(p))\) and \(\kappa(p)\) have the same rational numbers below them, and \(\varphi(\iota(p))=\kappa(p)\). In particular \(\varphi(0)=0\).

The third step shows that \(\varphi\) preserves order. Let \(x<y\). By density in \(\mathbb{F}\), there is a \(q\in\mathbb{Q}\) with \(x<\iota(q)<y\). By the first step, \(\kappa(q)<\varphi(y)\), and \(\kappa(q)\not<\varphi(x)\). So \(\varphi(x)\le\kappa(q)<\varphi(y)\). Conversely, if \(\varphi(x)<\varphi(y)\), then \(x<y\), since \(x=y\) and \(y<x\) are ruled out. In particular, \(\varphi\) is one-to-one.

The fourth step shows that \(\varphi\) is onto. Define \(\psi:\mathbb{G}\to\mathbb{F}\) in the same way, with the roles of \(\mathbb{F}\) and \(\mathbb{G}\) exchanged. By the first step for \(\psi\) and then for \(\varphi\), for every \(q\in\mathbb{Q}\), \[\iota(q)<\psi(\varphi(x))\iff\kappa(q)<\varphi(x)\iff\iota(q)<x.\] So \(\psi(\varphi(x))=x\) for every \(x\in\mathbb{F}\). In the same way, \(\varphi(\psi(g))=g\) for every \(g\in\mathbb{G}\). So \(\varphi\) maps \(\mathbb{F}\) onto \(\mathbb{G}\).

The fifth step shows that \(\varphi\) preserves sums. Let \(x,y\in\mathbb{F}\) and \(q\in\mathbb{Q}\). Suppose \(\kappa(q)<\varphi(x)+\varphi(y)\). Then \(\kappa(q)-\varphi(y)<\varphi(x)\). By density in \(\mathbb{G}\), there is an \(r\in\mathbb{Q}\) with \(\kappa(q)-\varphi(y)<\kappa(r)<\varphi(x)\). Then \(\kappa(q-r)<\varphi(y)\) and \(\kappa(r)<\varphi(x)\). By the first step, \(\iota(q-r)<y\) and \(\iota(r)<x\). Adding, \(\iota(q)<x+y\), and so \(\kappa(q)<\varphi(x+y)\) by the first step. The same argument in \(\mathbb{F}\) shows that \(\kappa(q)<\varphi(x+y)\) implies \(\kappa(q)<\varphi(x)+\varphi(y)\). So \(\varphi(x+y)=\varphi(x)+\varphi(y)\). In particular, \(\varphi(-x)=-\varphi(x)\), since \(\varphi(x)+\varphi(-x)=\varphi(0)=0\).

The sixth step shows that \(\varphi\) preserves products. First let \(x,y>0\). Then \(\varphi(x),\varphi(y)>0\) by the second and third steps. Let \(q\in\mathbb{Q}\). If \(q\le0\), then \(\kappa(q)\le0<\varphi(x)\varphi(y)\), and \(\iota(q)\le0<xy\), so \(\kappa(q)<\varphi(xy)\). Now let \(q>0\), and suppose \(\kappa(q)<\varphi(x)\varphi(y)\). Then \(0<\kappa(q)\varphi(y)^{-1}<\varphi(x)\). By density in \(\mathbb{G}\), there is an \(r\in\mathbb{Q}\) with \(\kappa(q)\varphi(y)^{-1}<\kappa(r)<\varphi(x)\). Then \(r>0\), \(\kappa(r)<\varphi(x)\) and \(\kappa\bigl(\frac qr\bigr)<\varphi(y)\). By the first step, \(0<\iota(r)<x\) and \(0<\iota\bigl(\frac qr\bigr)<y\). Multiplying, \(\iota(q)<xy\), so \(\kappa(q)<\varphi(xy)\). The same argument in \(\mathbb{F}\) gives the converse. So \(\varphi(xy)=\varphi(x)\varphi(y)\) when \(x,y>0\). The other cases follow from this one, since \(\varphi(0)=0\) and \(\varphi(-x)=-\varphi(x)\). For example, if \(x<0<y\), then \(\varphi(xy)=-\varphi((-x)y)=-\varphi(-x)\varphi(y)=\varphi(x)\varphi(y)\).

∎

Applied to the ordered fields of Appendices A and B, Theorem B.5.2 matches each cut with a class of Cauchy sequences. The cut of rational numbers below \(\sqrt2\) is matched with the class of every sequence of rational numbers that converges to \(\sqrt2\). Since the two constructions give copies of the same ordered field, the rest of the book never needs to know which one was used.

Notes

Charles Méray gave a construction of the real numbers from Cauchy sequences of rational numbers in 1869. Cantor gave his in 1872, in a paper on trigonometric series [8], the same year as Dedekind’s cuts. Eduard Heine published Cantor’s construction, with credit to Cantor, in the same year [38]. The same idea, applied to an arbitrary metric space instead of \(\mathbb{Q}\), gives the completion of a metric space. Theorem B.5.2 shows that the least upper bound property, together with the axioms of an ordered field, describes \(\mathbb{R}\) completely. Edward V. Huntington gave one of the first such complete sets of axioms in 1903.
