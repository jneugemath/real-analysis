This appendix proves Theorem 1.7.2. It builds an ordered field with the least upper bound property out of \(\mathbb{Q}\). The construction is due to Richard Dedekind.

The idea comes from Section 1.3. The rational numbers \(p\) with \(p\le0\) or \(p^2<2\) form a left piece of \(\mathbb{Q}\). The positive rational numbers with \(p^2>2\) form a right piece. No rational number sits between the two pieces. Dedekind’s idea was to let the left piece itself be the missing number. So a real number will be a set of rational numbers, namely all the rational numbers to its left. A rational number \(q\) gives such a set too, the set of all rational numbers less than \(q\).

Only \(\mathbb{Q}\) is assumed here, with its usual operations and order. So \(\mathbb{Q}\) is an ordered field (Example 1.5.7), and the results of Sections 1.4, 1.5 and 1.6 hold in \(\mathbb{Q}\). Nothing from Section 1.7 or later is used, since those results assume that \(\mathbb{R}\) exists. In this appendix the letters \(p\), \(q\), \(r\), \(s\), \(t\) and \(u\) always denote rational numbers, and Greek letters denote cuts.

A.1 Cuts

Definition A.1.1. A set \(\alpha\subset\mathbb{Q}\) is a cut if it has the following three properties.

  1. \(\alpha\ne\emptyset\) and \(\alpha\ne\mathbb{Q}\).

  2. If \(p\in\alpha\) and \(q<p\), then \(q\in\alpha\).

  3. \(\alpha\) has no largest element. That is, if \(p\in\alpha\), then \(p<r\) for some \(r\in\alpha\).

In this appendix, \(\mathbb{R}\) denotes the set of all cuts.

Remark A.1.2. Property (2) has two consequences that are used constantly. Let \(\alpha\) be a cut. If \(p\in\alpha\) and \(u\notin\alpha\), then \(p<u\). Indeed, \(u=p\) is impossible, and \(u<p\) would put \(u\) in \(\alpha\). Also, if \(u\notin\alpha\) and \(u<s\), then \(s\notin\alpha\). Indeed, \(s\in\alpha\) would put \(u\) in \(\alpha\). So everything below a member of \(\alpha\) is in \(\alpha\), and everything above a nonmember is outside \(\alpha\).

Example A.1.3. Let \(q\in\mathbb{Q}\). Then \(q^*=\{p\in\mathbb{Q}:p<q\}\) is a cut, called a rational cut. To see this, note that \(q-1\in q^*\) and \(q\notin q^*\). Property (2) is the transitivity of the order. For (3), if \(p<q\), then \(p<\frac{p+q}2<q\) by Exercise 1.3.3. Notice that \(q\) is the smallest element of \(\mathbb{Q}\setminus q^*\).

Example A.1.4. Let \(\alpha=\{p\in\mathbb{Q}:p\le0\text{ or }p^2<2\}\). Then \(\alpha\) is a cut, and \(\alpha\ne q^*\) for every \(q\in\mathbb{Q}\).

First, \(0\in\alpha\) and \(2\notin\alpha\). For (2), let \(p\in\alpha\) and \(q<p\). If \(q\le0\), then \(q\in\alpha\). If \(q>0\), then \(0<q<p\). So \(p^2<2\), and \(q^2<p^2<2\) by Exercise 1.5.3. So \(q\in\alpha\). For (3), let \(p\in\alpha\). If \(p\le0\), then \(1\in\alpha\) and \(1>p\). If \(p>0\), then \(p\) is in the set \(A\) of Theorem 1.3.5, which has no largest element. So \(\alpha\) is a cut.

The set \(\mathbb{Q}\setminus\alpha\) consists of the \(p>0\) with \(p^2\ge2\). By Theorem 1.3.4, \(p^2\ne2\). So \(\mathbb{Q}\setminus\alpha\) is the set \(B\) of Theorem 1.3.5, which has no smallest element. By Example A.1.3, \(\mathbb{Q}\setminus q^*\) has a smallest element. So \(\alpha\ne q^*\). This cut will play the role of \(\sqrt2\). Compare Figure 1.1.

Lemma A.1.5. Let \(p,r\in\mathbb{Q}\) with \(r>0\). Then there exists an \(n\in\mathbb{N}\) with \(nr>p\).

Proof.

If \(p\le0\), take \(n=1\). Suppose \(p>0\). As in the proof of Lemma 1.3.3, write \(p=\frac ab\) and \(r=\frac cd\) with \(a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{N}\). Let \(n=ad+1\). Since \(b\ge1\) and \(c\ge1\), \[nr=\frac{(ad+1)c}{d}\ge\frac{ad+1}{d}>a\ge\frac ab=p.\]

∎

Lemma A.1.6. Let \(\alpha\) be a cut and let \(r\in\mathbb{Q}\) with \(r>0\). Then there exists a \(p\in\alpha\) with \(p+r\notin\alpha\).

Proof.

Choose \(s\in\alpha\) and \(u\notin\alpha\). By Lemma A.1.5, \(nr>u-s\) for some \(n\in\mathbb{N}\). Then \(s+nr>u\), so \(s+nr\notin\alpha\) by Remark A.1.2. So the set \(S=\{n\in\mathbb{N}:s+nr\notin\alpha\}\) is not empty. By the Well-Ordering Principle, it has a smallest element \(n_0\). If \(n_0=1\), let \(p=s\). If \(n_0>1\), let \(p=s+(n_0-1)r\). Then \(p\in\alpha\), since \(n_0-1\notin S\). In both cases \(p\in\alpha\) and \(p+r=s+n_0r\notin\alpha\).

∎

In words, steps of size \(r\) that start inside \(\alpha\) must eventually leave it, and Lemma A.1.6 picks the last step inside. So the inside and the outside of a cut come within \(r\) of each other, for every \(r>0\). This lemma is the key to the negative of a cut in Section A.3 and to the reciprocal in Section A.4.

A.2 Order and the Least Upper Bound Property

Cuts are ordered by inclusion. A smaller real number has fewer rational numbers to its left.

Definition A.2.1. For cuts \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), define \(\alpha<\beta\) to mean \(\alpha\subsetneqq\beta\).

So \(\alpha\le\beta\) means \(\alpha\subset\beta\). For rational cuts, \(p^*<q^*\) exactly when \(p<q\), by Theorem A.5.1.

Theorem A.2.2. The relation \(<\) is an order on \(\mathbb{R}\).

Proof.

Transitivity. Let \(\alpha<\beta\) and \(\beta<\gamma\). Then \(\alpha\subset\beta\subset\gamma\), so \(\alpha\subset\gamma\). If \(\alpha=\gamma\), then \(\beta\subset\gamma=\alpha\subset\beta\), so \(\alpha=\beta\), which is false. So \(\alpha<\gamma\).

Trichotomy. At most one of \(\alpha<\beta\), \(\alpha=\beta\) and \(\beta<\alpha\) holds. Indeed, \(\alpha<\beta\) and \(\beta<\alpha\) would give \(\alpha\subset\beta\subset\alpha\), so \(\alpha=\beta\), which is false. Now suppose \(\alpha\ne\beta\) and \(\alpha\not<\beta\). Then \(\alpha\not\subset\beta\). So there is a \(p\in\alpha\) with \(p\notin\beta\). Let \(q\in\beta\). Since \(p\notin\beta\), \(q<p\) by Remark A.1.2. Since \(p\in\alpha\), \(q\in\alpha\). So \(\beta\subset\alpha\). Since \(\beta\ne\alpha\), \(\beta<\alpha\).

∎

Theorem A.2.3. The ordered set \(\mathbb{R}\) has the least upper bound property. If \(A\subset\mathbb{R}\) is nonempty and bounded above, then \(\sup A\) is the union of the cuts in \(A\).

Proof.

Let \(\beta\) be an upper bound of \(A\), and let \(\gamma=\bigcup_{\alpha\in A}\alpha\).

The set \(\gamma\) is a cut. Choose \(\alpha_0\in A\). Then \(\emptyset\ne\alpha_0\subset\gamma\). Each \(\alpha\in A\) has \(\alpha\le\beta\), that is, \(\alpha\subset\beta\). So \(\gamma\subset\beta\ne\mathbb{Q}\), and \(\gamma\ne\mathbb{Q}\). For (2), let \(p\in\gamma\) and \(q<p\). Then \(p\in\alpha\) for some \(\alpha\in A\). So \(q\in\alpha\subset\gamma\). For (3), let \(p\in\gamma\), say \(p\in\alpha\) with \(\alpha\in A\). Then \(p<r\) for some \(r\in\alpha\), and \(r\in\gamma\).

The cut \(\gamma\) is an upper bound of \(A\). Each \(\alpha\in A\) satisfies \(\alpha\subset\gamma\), so \(\alpha\le\gamma\).

No smaller cut is an upper bound of \(A\). Let \(\delta<\gamma\). Then there is a \(p\in\gamma\) with \(p\notin\delta\). Say \(p\in\alpha\) with \(\alpha\in A\). Then \(\alpha\not\subset\delta\), so \(\alpha\not\le\delta\). By Theorem A.2.2, \(\delta<\alpha\). So \(\delta\) is not an upper bound of \(A\). Thus \(\gamma=\sup A\).

∎

Notice how little work this took. The hole in \(\mathbb{Q}\) at \(\sqrt2\) was a gap between a left piece and a right piece. A cut is a left piece, so the gap is filled by definition. The real work in this appendix is the arithmetic.

A.3 Addition

Definition A.3.1. For cuts \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), define \[\alpha+\beta=\{r+s:r\in\alpha,\ s\in\beta\}.\] Let \(0^*=\{p\in\mathbb{Q}:p<0\}\), the rational cut of \(0\).

Lemma A.3.2. If \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are cuts, then \(\alpha+\beta\) is a cut.

Proof.

Clearly \(\alpha+\beta\ne\emptyset\). Choose \(u\notin\alpha\) and \(t\notin\beta\). If \(r\in\alpha\) and \(s\in\beta\), then \(r<u\) and \(s<t\), so \(r+s<u+t\). So \(u+t\notin\alpha+\beta\), and \(\alpha+\beta\ne\mathbb{Q}\). For (2), let \(p=r+s\) with \(r\in\alpha\) and \(s\in\beta\), and let \(q<p\). Then \(q-s<r\), so \(q-s\in\alpha\), and \(q=(q-s)+s\in\alpha+\beta\). For (3), choose \(r'\in\alpha\) with \(r'>r\). Then \(r'+s\in\alpha+\beta\) and \(r'+s>p\).

∎

Definition A.3.3. For a cut \(\alpha\), let \(-\alpha\) be the set of all \(p\in\mathbb{Q}\) such that \(-p-r\notin\alpha\) for some rational \(r>0\).

In words, \(p\in-\alpha\) when \(-p\) lies outside \(\alpha\) with some room to spare. For a rational cut, \(-(q^*)=(-q)^*\). Indeed, \(p\in-(q^*)\) means \(-p-r\ge q\) for some \(r>0\), which happens exactly when \(-p>q\), that is, \(p<-q\). The room to spare matters. Without it, \(-(q^*)\) would contain \(-q\) as a largest element.

Theorem A.3.4. Let \(\alpha\), \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\) be cuts.

  1. \(\alpha+\beta=\beta+\alpha\).

  2. \((\alpha+\beta)+\gamma=\alpha+(\beta+\gamma)\).

  3. \(\alpha+0^*=\alpha\).

  4. \(-\alpha\) is a cut, and \(\alpha+(-\alpha)=0^*\).

  5. If \(\beta\le\gamma\), then \(\alpha+\beta\le\alpha+\gamma\).

Proof.

Parts (1) and (2) are Exercise A.3.1. Part (5) holds since \(\beta\subset\gamma\) gives \(\alpha+\beta\subset\alpha+\gamma\).

(3) Let \(r\in\alpha\) and \(s\in0^*\). Then \(r+s<r\), so \(r+s\in\alpha\). So \(\alpha+0^*\subset\alpha\). Conversely, let \(p\in\alpha\). Choose \(r\in\alpha\) with \(r>p\). Then \(p-r\in0^*\), and \(p=r+(p-r)\in\alpha+0^*\). So \(\alpha\subset\alpha+0^*\).

(4) First, \(-\alpha\) is a cut. Choose \(u\notin\alpha\), and let \(p=-u-1\). Then \(-p-1=u\notin\alpha\), so \(p\in-\alpha\). Next, let \(q\in\alpha\). For every \(r>0\), \(-(-q)-r=q-r<q\), so \(q-r\in\alpha\). So \(-q\notin-\alpha\), and \(-\alpha\ne\mathbb{Q}\). For (2), let \(p\in-\alpha\), with \(-p-r\notin\alpha\), and let \(q<p\). Then \(-q-r>-p-r\), so \(-q-r\notin\alpha\) by Remark A.1.2. So \(q\in-\alpha\). For (3), let \(p\in-\alpha\), with \(-p-r\notin\alpha\). Let \(t=p+\frac r2\). Then \(-t-\frac r2=-p-r\notin\alpha\), so \(t\in-\alpha\), and \(t>p\).

Next, \(\alpha+(-\alpha)\subset0^*\). Let \(r\in\alpha\) and \(s\in-\alpha\), with \(-s-t\notin\alpha\) for some \(t>0\). Then \(r<-s-t\), so \(r+s<-t<0\).

Finally, \(0^*\subset\alpha+(-\alpha)\). Let \(v\in0^*\), and let \(w=-\frac v2>0\). By Lemma A.1.6, there is a \(p\in\alpha\) with \(p+w\notin\alpha\). Let \(q=-p-2w\). Then \(-q-w=p+w\notin\alpha\), so \(q\in-\alpha\). And \(p+q=-2w=v\). So \(v\in\alpha+(-\alpha)\).

∎

Proof Technique (Proving Two Cuts Are Equal).

Cuts are sets, so two cuts are equal when each is contained in the other. Usually one inclusion is easy, and it follows from the order of \(\mathbb{Q}\), as in the first half of the proof of Theorem A.3.4 (3). The other inclusion needs room to move. Given \(p\) in one cut, it must be written as a sum or a product of elements of the other cuts. Property (3) of Definition A.1.1, or Lemma A.1.6, gives the room.

Corollary A.3.5. Axioms (1)–(5) of Definition 1.5.1 hold for cuts, with \(0^*\) as the zero. So does property (1) of Definition 1.5.6. Therefore Theorem 1.5.5 (1)–(4) and Theorem 1.5.8 (1) hold for cuts, since their proofs use only these facts. In particular, \(-\alpha\) is the only cut with \(\alpha+(-\alpha)=0^*\), \(-(-\alpha)=\alpha\), and \(\alpha>0^*\) if and only if \(-\alpha<0^*\).

Proof.

Axiom (1) is Lemma A.3.2. Axioms (2)–(5) are Theorem A.3.4 (1)–(4), using (1) to write \(0^*+\alpha=\alpha\). Property (1) of Definition 1.5.6 is Theorem A.3.4 (5), together with (1).

∎

A.4 Multiplication

Multiplying left pieces is harder than adding them, because multiplying by a negative number reverses the order. So multiplication is defined first for positive cuts. Then it is extended to all cuts by the usual rules of signs.

Lemma A.4.1. Let \(\alpha\) be a cut with \(\alpha>0^*\). Then \(0\in\alpha\), \(\alpha\) contains a positive rational number, and every \(u\notin\alpha\) is positive.

Proof.

Since \(0^*\subsetneqq\alpha\), there is a \(p\in\alpha\) with \(p\notin0^*\), that is, \(p\ge0\). So \(0\in\alpha\). By property (3) of Definition A.1.1, some \(r\in\alpha\) has \(r>0\). If \(u\notin\alpha\), then \(u>0\) by Remark A.1.2, since \(0\in\alpha\).

∎

Definition A.4.2. For a cut \(\alpha>0^*\), let \(\alpha^+=\{p\in\alpha:p>0\}\). For cuts \(\alpha,\beta>0^*\), define \(\alpha\beta\) to be the set of all \(p\in\mathbb{Q}\) such that \[p\le rs\quad\text{for some }r\in\alpha^+\text{ and }s\in\beta^+.\] Also define \(\alpha^{-1}\) to be the set of all \(p\in\mathbb{Q}\) such that \(p\le0\), or \(p>0\) and \(pu<1\) for some \(u\notin\alpha\). Let \(1^*=\{p\in\mathbb{Q}:p<1\}\), the rational cut of \(1\).

The definition of \(\alpha^{-1}\) follows the definition of \(-\alpha\). A positive \(p\) is in \(\alpha^{-1}\) when \(\frac1p\) lies outside \(\alpha\) with room to spare, namely when \(\frac1p>u\) for some \(u\notin\alpha\). For example, if \(q>0\), then \((q^*)^{-1}=\bigl(\frac1q\bigr)^*\).

Lemma A.4.3. Let \(\alpha,\beta>0^*\). Then \(\alpha\beta\) and \(\alpha^{-1}\) are cuts, \(\alpha\beta>0^*\), and \(\alpha^{-1}>0^*\).

Proof.

The product. By Lemma A.4.1, \(\alpha^+\) and \(\beta^+\) are not empty, so \(\alpha\beta\ne\emptyset\). Choose \(u\notin\alpha\) and \(t\notin\beta\). Both are positive. If \(r\in\alpha^+\) and \(s\in\beta^+\), then \(0<r<u\) and \(0<s<t\), so \(rs<ut\) by Theorem 1.5.8 (3). So \(ut\notin\alpha\beta\), and \(\alpha\beta\ne\mathbb{Q}\). Property (2) holds, since \(q<p\le rs\) gives \(q\le rs\). For (3), let \(p\le rs\) with \(r\in\alpha^+\) and \(s\in\beta^+\). Choose \(r'\in\alpha\) with \(r'>r\). Then \(r's\in\alpha\beta\) and \(r's>rs\ge p\). Finally, \(0\le rs\), so \(0\in\alpha\beta\setminus0^*\). Every negative number is less than \(rs\), so \(0^*\subset\alpha\beta\). Thus \(\alpha\beta>0^*\).

The reciprocal. Clearly \(0\in\alpha^{-1}\). Let \(t\in\alpha^+\). If \(u\notin\alpha\), then \(u>t\), so \(\frac1t\cdot u>1\). So \(\frac1t\notin\alpha^{-1}\), and \(\alpha^{-1}\ne\mathbb{Q}\). For (2), let \(p\in\alpha^{-1}\) and \(q<p\). If \(q\le0\), then \(q\in\alpha^{-1}\). If \(q>0\), then \(p>0\) and \(pu<1\) for some \(u\notin\alpha\). Since \(u>0\), \(qu<pu<1\), so \(q\in\alpha^{-1}\). For (3), let \(p\in\alpha^{-1}\). If \(p\le0\), choose \(u\notin\alpha\). Then \(\frac1{2u}>0\ge p\) and \(\frac1{2u}\cdot u<1\), so \(\frac1{2u}\in\alpha^{-1}\). If \(p>0\), then \(p<\frac1u\) for some \(u\notin\alpha\). Let \(p'=\frac12\bigl(p+\frac1u\bigr)\). Then \(p<p'<\frac1u\), so \(p'u<1\) and \(p'\in\alpha^{-1}\). Thus \(\alpha^{-1}\) is a cut. It contains \(0\), so \(\alpha^{-1}>0^*\).

∎

Lemma A.4.4. Let \(\alpha>0^*\) and let \(t\in\mathbb{Q}\) with \(t>1\). Then there exists a \(p\in\alpha^+\) with \(pt\notin\alpha\).

Proof.

Choose \(p_0\in\alpha^+\), and let \(r=p_0(t-1)>0\). By Lemma A.1.6, there is a \(p_1\in\alpha\) with \(p_1+r\notin\alpha\). Let \(p=\max\{p_0,p_1\}\). Then \(p\in\alpha^+\). Since \(p\ge p_1\), \(p+r\notin\alpha\). Since \(p\ge p_0\), \[pt=p+p(t-1)\ge p+p_0(t-1)=p+r.\] So \(pt\notin\alpha\) by Remark A.1.2.

∎

Theorem A.4.5. Let \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma>0^*\).

  1. \(\alpha\beta=\beta\alpha\).

  2. \((\alpha\beta)\gamma=\alpha(\beta\gamma)\).

  3. \(1^*\alpha=\alpha\).

  4. \(\alpha\alpha^{-1}=1^*\).

  5. \(\alpha(\beta+\gamma)=\alpha\beta+\alpha\gamma\).

Proof.

(1) The definition of \(\alpha\beta\) is symmetric in \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), since \(rs=sr\).

(2) Let \(p\in(\alpha\beta)\gamma\). Then \(p\le wt\) with \(w\in(\alpha\beta)^+\) and \(t\in\gamma^+\), and \(w\le rs\) with \(r\in\alpha^+\) and \(s\in\beta^+\). So \(p\le rst\). Conversely, if \(p\le rst\) with \(r\in\alpha^+\), \(s\in\beta^+\) and \(t\in\gamma^+\), then \(rs\in(\alpha\beta)^+\), so \(p\in(\alpha\beta)\gamma\). So \((\alpha\beta)\gamma\) is the set of \(p\) with \(p\le rst\) for some \(r\in\alpha^+\), \(s\in\beta^+\) and \(t\in\gamma^+\). The same argument gives the same description of \(\alpha(\beta\gamma)\).

(3) Let \(p\in1^*\alpha\). Then \(p\le rs\) with \(0<r<1\) and \(s\in\alpha^+\). So \(p<s\), and \(p\in\alpha\). Conversely, let \(p\in\alpha\). Choose \(s_0\in\alpha^+\). Then \(\max\{p,s_0\}\in\alpha\), so there is an \(s\in\alpha\) with \(s>\max\{p,s_0\}\). In particular \(s>0\). If \(p\le0\), then \(p\le\frac12s\) with \(\frac12\in(1^*)^+\). If \(p>0\), then \(r=\frac ps\) satisfies \(0<r<1\) and \(p=rs\). Either way \(p\in1^*\alpha\).

(4) Let \(p\in\alpha\alpha^{-1}\). Then \(p\le rs\) with \(r\in\alpha^+\), \(s>0\) and \(su<1\) for some \(u\notin\alpha\). Since \(r<u\), \(rs<us<1\). So \(p<1\), and \(p\in1^*\). Conversely, let \(q\in1^*\). If \(q\le0\), then \(q\in\alpha\alpha^{-1}\), since \(\alpha\alpha^{-1}>0^*\). Let \(0<q<1\), and let \(t=\frac12\bigl(1+\frac1q\bigr)\). Then \(1<t<\frac1q\). By Lemma A.4.4, there is a \(p\in\alpha^+\) with \(pt\notin\alpha\). Let \(s=\frac qp>0\). Then \(s\cdot pt=qt<1\) and \(pt\notin\alpha\), so \(s\in\alpha^{-1}\). Since \(q=ps\), \(q\in\alpha\alpha^{-1}\).

(5) First, \(\beta+\gamma>0^*\), since \(\beta+\gamma\ge0^*+\gamma=\gamma>0^*\) by Theorem A.3.4. Next, every \(w\in\beta+\gamma\) satisfies \(w\le s+t\) for some \(s\in\beta^+\) and \(t\in\gamma^+\). Indeed, \(w=s_1+t_1\) with \(s_1\in\beta\) and \(t_1\in\gamma\). Choose \(s_0\in\beta^+\) and \(t_0\in\gamma^+\), and let \(s=\max\{s_0,s_1\}\) and \(t=\max\{t_0,t_1\}\).

Let \(p\in\alpha(\beta+\gamma)\). Then \(p\le rw\) with \(r\in\alpha^+\) and \(w\in(\beta+\gamma)^+\). Choose \(s\in\beta^+\) and \(t\in\gamma^+\) with \(w\le s+t\). Then \(p\le rs+rt\). Since \(rs\in\alpha\beta\) and \(rt\in\alpha\gamma\), \(rs+rt\in\alpha\beta+\alpha\gamma\), and so \(p\in\alpha\beta+\alpha\gamma\).

Conversely, let \(p\in\alpha\beta+\alpha\gamma\). Then \(p=x+y\) with \(x\le rs\) and \(y\le r't\), where \(r,r'\in\alpha^+\), \(s\in\beta^+\) and \(t\in\gamma^+\). Let \(R=\max\{r,r'\}\in\alpha^+\). Then \[p\le rs+r't\le Rs+Rt=R(s+t).\] Since \(s+t\in(\beta+\gamma)^+\), \(p\in\alpha(\beta+\gamma)\).

∎

Definition A.4.6. For cuts \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) that are not both positive, define \[\alpha\beta=\begin{cases} (-\alpha)(-\beta), & \alpha<0^*\text{ and }\beta<0^*,\\ -\bigl((-\alpha)\beta\bigr), & \alpha<0^*<\beta,\\ -\bigl(\alpha(-\beta)\bigr), & \beta<0^*<\alpha,\\ 0^*, & \alpha=0^*\text{ or }\beta=0^*. \end{cases}\] For \(\alpha<0^*\), define \(\alpha^{-1}=-\bigl((-\alpha)^{-1}\bigr)\).

If \(\alpha<0^*\), then \(-\alpha>0^*\) by Corollary A.3.5. So every product on the right side is a product of positive cuts, from Definition A.4.2. These are the familiar rules of signs.

Theorem A.4.7. With the addition of Definition A.3.1, the multiplication of Definitions A.4.2 and A.4.6, and the order of Definition A.2.1, \(\mathbb{R}\) is an ordered field. Its zero is \(0^*\) and its one is \(1^*\).

Proof.

Axioms (1)–(5) of Definition 1.5.1 hold by Corollary A.3.5. Every product of cuts is a cut, by Lemma A.4.3, Theorem A.3.4 (4) and Definition A.4.6. So (6) holds. Each case of Definition A.4.6 is symmetric in \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), by Theorem A.4.5 (1). So (7) holds.

For (9), note \(1^*\ne0^*\), since \(0\in1^*\) and \(0\notin0^*\). If \(\alpha>0^*\), then \(1^*\alpha=\alpha\) by Theorem A.4.5 (3). If \(\alpha<0^*\), then \(1^*\alpha=-\bigl(1^*(-\alpha)\bigr)=-(-\alpha)=\alpha\). If \(\alpha=0^*\), then \(1^*\alpha=0^*=\alpha\).

For (10), let \(\alpha\ne0^*\). If \(\alpha>0^*\), then \(\alpha\alpha^{-1}=1^*\) by Theorem A.4.5 (4). If \(\alpha<0^*\), then \((-\alpha)^{-1}>0^*\), so \(\alpha^{-1}<0^*\). So \(\alpha\alpha^{-1}=(-\alpha)\bigl(-\alpha^{-1}\bigr)=(-\alpha)(-\alpha)^{-1}=1^*\).

Axioms (8) and (11) follow from Theorem A.4.5 (2) and (5), by splitting into cases by signs. This is Exercise A.4.1.

Property (1) of Definition 1.5.6 holds by Corollary A.3.5. For (2), let \(0^*\le\alpha\) and \(0^*\le\beta\). If \(\alpha=0^*\) or \(\beta=0^*\), then \(\alpha\beta=0^*\). Otherwise \(\alpha\beta>0^*\) by Lemma A.4.3.

∎

A.5 The Rational Numbers as a Subfield

It remains to find \(\mathbb{Q}\) inside \(\mathbb{R}\). Each rational number \(q\) has its rational cut \(q^*\). The next theorem shows that the rational cuts add, multiply and compare exactly as the rational numbers do.

Theorem A.5.1. Let \(p,q\in\mathbb{Q}\).

  1. \(p<q\) if and only if \(p^*<q^*\).

  2. \((p+q)^*=p^*+q^*\).

  3. \((-q)^*=-(q^*)\).

  4. \((pq)^*=p^*q^*\).

Proof.

(1) Let \(p<q\). If \(r\in p^*\), then \(r<p<q\), so \(r\in q^*\). Also \(p\in q^*\) and \(p\notin p^*\). So \(p^*<q^*\). Conversely, let \(p^*<q^*\). Then \(p\ne q\). If \(q<p\), then \(q^*<p^*\) by the first half, which contradicts trichotomy. So \(p<q\).

(2) If \(r<p\) and \(s<q\), then \(r+s<p+q\). So \(p^*+q^*\subset(p+q)^*\). Conversely, let \(t<p+q\), and let \(h=\frac12(p+q-t)>0\). Then \(p-h\in p^*\), \(q-h\in q^*\), and \((p-h)+(q-h)=t\). So \(t\in p^*+q^*\).

(3) By (2), \(q^*+(-q)^*=0^*\). By Corollary A.3.5, \((-q)^*=-(q^*)\).

(4) First let \(p,q>0\). Then \(p^*,q^*>0^*\) by (1), and \((p^*)^+=\{r:0<r<p\}\). If \(r\in(p^*)^+\) and \(s\in(q^*)^+\), then \(rs<pq\). So every element of \(p^*q^*\) is less than \(pq\), and \(p^*q^*\subset(pq)^*\). Conversely, let \(t<pq\). If \(t\le0\), then \(t\le\frac p2\cdot\frac q2\), so \(t\in p^*q^*\). If \(t>0\), let \(k=\frac t{pq}\) and \(c=\frac12(1+k)\), so that \(0<k<c<1\). Let \(r=cp\) and \(s=\frac tr=\frac{kq}c\). Then \(0<r<p\), \(0<s<q\) and \(rs=t\). So \(t\in p^*q^*\).

If \(p=0\) or \(q=0\), both sides of (4) are \(0^*\). Suppose \(p<0<q\). Then \(p^*<0^*<q^*\) by (1), and \(-(p^*)=(-p)^*\) by (3). By Definition A.4.6, the positive case and (3), \[p^*q^*=-\bigl((-p)^*q^*\bigr)=-\bigl(((-p)q)^*\bigr)=-\bigl((-(pq))^*\bigr)=(pq)^*.\] The cases \(p>0>q\) and \(p,q<0\) are Exercise A.5.2.

∎

Theorem A.5.2. Let \(\mathbb{Q}^*=\{q^*:q\in\mathbb{Q}\}\). The map \(q\mapsto q^*\) is one-to-one from \(\mathbb{Q}\) onto \(\mathbb{Q}^*\), and \(\mathbb{Q}^*\) is a subfield of \(\mathbb{R}\).

Proof.

If \(p\ne q\), then \(p<q\) or \(q<p\), so \(p^*\ne q^*\) by Theorem A.5.1 (1). By Theorem A.5.1, \(\mathbb{Q}^*\) contains \(0^*\) and \(1^*\), and it contains the sum, the product and the negative of any of its elements. If \(q\ne0\), then \(q^*\bigl(\frac1q\bigr)^*=1^*\) by Theorem A.5.1 (4), so \((q^*)^{-1}=\bigl(\frac1q\bigr)^*\in\mathbb{Q}^*\) by Theorem 1.5.5 (7). The axioms of an ordered field hold in \(\mathbb{Q}^*\), since they hold in \(\mathbb{R}\). So \(\mathbb{Q}^*\), with the operations and order of \(\mathbb{R}\), is an ordered field contained in \(\mathbb{R}\).

∎

So \(\mathbb{Q}^*\) is a perfect copy of \(\mathbb{Q}\) inside \(\mathbb{R}\). Every sum, product and inequality of rational numbers matches the same statement about their cuts. From now on, identify each \(q\in\mathbb{Q}\) with its cut \(q^*\). Then \(\mathbb{Q}\subset\mathbb{R}\), and \(\mathbb{Q}\), with its usual operations and order, is a subfield of \(\mathbb{R}\).

Strictly speaking, \(q\) and \(q^*\) are different objects. The number \(q\) is a fraction, and \(q^*\) is a set of fractions. But nothing in this book depends on what a real number is made of. It depends only on how real numbers add, multiply and compare, and in those ways \(q\) and \(q^*\) are the same.

Proof of Theorem 1.7.2.

By Theorem A.4.7, \(\mathbb{R}\) is an ordered field. By Theorem A.2.3, it has the least upper bound property. By Theorem A.5.2, after each \(q\in\mathbb{Q}\) is identified with \(q^*\), \(\mathbb{Q}\) is a subfield of \(\mathbb{R}\).

∎

Notes

Richard Dedekind found the idea of a cut in 1858, while teaching calculus in Zurich. He did not want to rely on geometric intuition for the completeness of the line. He published the construction in 1872 in Stetigkeit und irrationale Zahlen [20]. Dedekind’s cuts were pairs of sets, a left piece and a right piece. The version here keeps only the left piece and does not allow it a largest element, as in Rudin [61].
