Preface

Who this book is for

This book is for a reader wanting to learn real analysis from the very beginning. It covers introductory real analysis through measure theory and Lebesgue integrals. It does not assume a course in proofs. A reader who has finished calculus and is willing to work should be able to read it.

Most students encounter two difficulties when taking real analysis. The ideas are new, and so is writing proofs. Many textbooks assume the second problem was solved in an earlier course. This book does not. Chapter 1 starts with sets and the integers. Each proof technique is explained the first time it is used. By the time the \(\varepsilon\)-\(N\) definition of convergence arrives in Chapter 3, the reader has already seen direct proofs, proofs by contradiction, proofs by cases and induction, all on simpler material.

The book is also meant to be complete. Results are proven, not quoted. When a fact is assumed, the text says so. The goal is to give a student a book that can be read without a lecture to fill in the gaps.

How the book is organized

The book has three parts. Part I, Numbers, Limits and Derivatives, starts from the axioms for the real numbers and goes through sequences, the topology of the real line, continuity and derivatives. Part II, Riemann Integrals and Series, covers functions of bounded variation, the Riemann and Riemann–Stieltjes integrals, series, and sequences and series of functions. Parts I and II are written for a two-semester undergraduate sequence. At Eastern Kentucky University that sequence is MAT 315, which covers Part I (Chapters 1–7), and MAT 415, which covers Part II (Chapters 8–12).

Part III, Measure and Lebesgue Integrals, covers measure theory and the Lebesgue integral. It is written for a first graduate course in real analysis, MAT 815 at Eastern Kentucky University, which sadly no longer exists. It assumes Parts I and II.

Appendices A and B construct the real numbers from the rational numbers in two different ways. Each one proves Theorem 1.7.2, which Chapter 1 uses as a starting point. They can be read any time after Chapter 1. Appendix B also uses the idea of a Cauchy sequence from Section 3.5.

Chapters and sections marked with a star (\(\ast\)) can be skipped. They are Section 2.4, Chapter 5, Sections 6.6, 7.3 and 7.4, Chapter 8, and Sections 9.4, 9.5, 9.8, 9.9, 10.4, 11.3, 11.4, 11.5, 12.4 and 12.5 in Parts I and II, and Sections 14.4, 15.4, 17.1, 17.6 and 17.7 and Chapter 18 in Part III, and Section B.5 in Appendix B. Nothing else in the book depends on these sections, with a few small exceptions. Example 9.2.8 uses the Cantor set. Chapter 17 uses the statement of the Vitali Covering Lemma from Section 17.1, but not its proof. Section 9.9 uses functions of bounded variation from Chapter 8, and Sections 17.3 and 17.5 recall its definitions and use a few of its results. Partitions are defined again in Section 9.1, so Chapter 9 can be read without Chapter 8. Appendices A and B construct \(\mathbb{R}\) from \(\mathbb{Q}\) in two ways. They can also be skipped. Sections 9.4, 9.5, 12.4 and 12.5 build \(\ln x\), \(e^x\), \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\), two ways each. The rest of the book uses these functions freely, so a reader who skips those sections should assume their familiar properties, as Remark 3.1.1 does.

How to use this book

Read the Proof Technique boxes. They are listed after the Contents, so they can be referenced again later. Every section ends with exercises. Trying these exercises is the only way to learn to write proofs. Exercises marked [Hint] or [Solution] have hints or solutions in Appendix C. Always try problems before reading the hints and solutions. The Notation list gives the page where each symbol is introduced.

Sources

This book grew out of lecture notes for MAT 315, MAT 415 and MAT 815 at Eastern Kentucky University. The notes for Parts I and II were adapted from Rudin’s Principles of Mathematical Analysis [61] and Stoll’s Introduction to Real Analysis [70]. The notes for Part III were adapted from Folland’s Real Analysis [30], Royden’s Real Analysis [60] and Rudin’s Real and Complex Analysis [62]. Many definitions and proofs here follow their treatments.

Use of AI

The definitions, theorems, proofs, examples and exercises in this book began as my lecture notes. They were written and typed before any AI tool was used. After that, I used Claude Opus 5.5, an AI model made by Anthropic, to help turn the notes into a book. It standardized the notation and cross-references, found and fixed errors, and filled in missing steps in proofs. It suggested where to insert Proof Technique boxes. It also drafted new material. This includes some exposition, the Notes at the end of each chapter, the figures, and some theorems, examples and exercises. I reviewed and revised all of this material. I am responsible for every word, and for any errors that remain.
