Solutions for Chapter 12. Power Series Expansions

Exercise 12.1.1. Hint. Set \(y=(x+1)^2\). The series becomes \(\sum\left(\frac y4\right)^k\).

Solution. The radius of convergence is \(R=2\). Write the series as \(\sum_{k=0}^\infty a_k(x+1)^k\). Then \(a_{2j}=\frac1{4^j}\) and \(a_k=0\) for odd \(k\). For \(k=2j\), \[\sqrt[k]{|a_k|}=\left(4^{-j}\right)^{1/(2j)}=\frac12,\] and for odd \(k\), \(\sqrt[k]{|a_k|}=0\). So \(\sup\left\{\sqrt[k]{|a_k|}:k\ge n\right\}=\frac12\) for every \(n\), and \(\limsup\sqrt[k]{|a_k|}=\frac12\). By Definition 12.1.4, \(R=2\).

This agrees with the substitution \(y=(x+1)^2\). The series becomes the geometric series \(\sum\left(\frac y4\right)^k\), which converges exactly when \((x+1)^2<4\), that is, \(-3<x<1\). At \(x=-3\) and \(x=1\) every term is \(1\), so the series diverges there.

Exercise 12.1.4. Hint. Follow the proof of the right inequality. Take \(0<\beta<\liminf\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}\) and show that \(a_k\ge c\beta^k\) for large \(k\), for some \(c>0\).

Solution. Let \(\gamma=\liminf\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}\). If \(\gamma=0\), there is nothing to prove, since every \(\sqrt[k]{a_k}>0\). Otherwise let \(0<b<\gamma\). By Definition 3.6.8, \(\gamma=\sup_n\inf\left\{\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}:k\ge n\right\}\). Since \(b<\gamma\), there is a \(K\in\mathbb{N}\) with \(\inf\left\{\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}:k\ge K\right\}>b\). So \(a_{k+1}\ge ba_k\) for all \(k\ge K\). By induction, \(a_k\ge a_Kb^{k-K}\) for all \(k\ge K\). So for \(k\ge K\), \[\sqrt[k]{a_k}\ge z_k,\quad\text{where}\quad z_k=\sqrt[k]{a_Kb^{-K}}\cdot b.\] Since \(a_Kb^{-K}>0\), \(z_k\to b\) by Lemma 12.1.3. For \(n\ge K\), each element of \(\{\sqrt[k]{a_k}:k\ge n\}\) is at least the matching \(z_k\). So \(\inf\{\sqrt[k]{a_k}:k\ge n\}\ge\inf\{z_k:k\ge n\}\). Letting \(n\to\infty\) gives \(\liminf\sqrt[k]{a_k}\ge\liminf z_k=b\), by Corollary 3.6.14. Since this holds for every \(b<\gamma\), \(\liminf\sqrt[k]{a_k}\ge\gamma\). This also covers \(\gamma=\infty\).

Exercise 12.2.1. Hint. Fix \(\rho\) with \(|x-c|\le\rho<R\). The series for \(f\) converges uniformly for \(|t-c|\le\rho\). Integrate it term by term, as in Example 12.2.2. (a) Expand \(\frac1{1+t^2}\) as a geometric series.

Solution. Fix \(x\) with \(|x-c|<R\), and choose \(\rho\) with \(|x-c|\le\rho<R\). By Theorem 12.1.7 (3), \(\sum a_k(t-c)^k\) converges uniformly to \(f(t)\) for \(|t-c|\le\rho\). In particular, it converges uniformly on the closed interval between \(c\) and \(x\). Each term is continuous, so integrable. Suppose \(x>c\). By Corollary 11.2.19 and the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, \[F(x)=\int_c^xf(t)\,dt=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\int_c^xa_k(t-c)^k\,dt=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{a_k}{k+1}(x-c)^{k+1}.\] If \(x<c\), apply Corollary 11.2.19 on \([x,c]\) and multiply by \(-1\), using Theorem 10.2.4 (1). Since \(\int_x^ca_k(t-c)^k\,dt=-\frac{a_k}{k+1}(x-c)^{k+1}\), the same formula results. If \(x=c\), both sides are \(0\).

(a) For \(|t|<1\), the geometric series with ratio \(-t^2\) gives \(\frac{1}{1+t^2}=\sum_{k=0}^\infty(-1)^kt^{2k}\). This is a power series in \(t\) whose coefficients are \((-1)^k\) for the power \(t^{2k}\) and \(0\) for odd powers. As in Exercise 12.1.1, \(\limsup\sqrt[m]{|a_m|}=1\), so its radius of convergence is \(1\). By the first part, for \(|x|<1\), \[\int_0^x\frac{dt}{1+t^2}=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^k}{2k+1}x^{2k+1}.\] Since \(\frac{d}{dx}\arctan x=\frac{1}{1+x^2}\) and \(\arctan0=0\), the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus gives \(\int_0^x\frac{dt}{1+t^2}=\arctan x\). (For \(x<0\), \(\int_0^x\) means \(-\int_x^0\).) So \(\arctan x=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^k}{2k+1}x^{2k+1}\) for \(x\in(-1,1)\).

(b) Let \(g(x)=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^k}{2k+1}x^{2k+1}=\sum_{m=0}^\infty b_mx^m\), where \(b_{2k+1}=\frac{(-1)^k}{2k+1}\) and \(b_m=0\) for even \(m\). For odd \(m\), \(\sqrt[m]{|b_m|}=\frac{1}{\sqrt[m]{m}}\), which is at most \(1\) and tends to \(1\) by Lemma 12.1.3. So every tail of \(\{\sqrt[m]{|b_m|}\}\) has supremum \(1\), and the radius of convergence is \(1\). The series \(\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^k}{2k+1}\) converges by the Alternating Series Test, since \(\frac1{2k+1}\) decreases to \(0\). The partial sums of \(\sum b_m\) are \(0\) followed by the partial sums of \(\sum\frac{(-1)^k}{2k+1}\), each listed twice. So \(\sum b_m\) converges to the same sum. By Abel’s Theorem (Theorem 12.2.1) and (a), \[\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^k}{2k+1}=\lim_{x\to1^-}g(x)=\lim_{x\to1^-}\arctan x=\arctan1=\frac\pi4,\] since \(\arctan\) is continuous. So the formula in (a) holds at \(x=1\). At \(x=-1\), the series is \(\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^k(-1)^{2k+1}}{2k+1}=-\frac\pi4=\arctan(-1)\). So it holds at \(x=-1\) too.

Exercise 12.3.2. Hint. Use the Taylor polynomial of \(\ln(1+x)\) at \(0\) with \(x=0.2\). By Theorem 12.3.7, the error is at most \(\frac{(0.2)^4}{4}\).

Solution. Let \(f(x)=\ln(1+x)\) on \(I=(-1,\infty)\), with \(c=0\). By Example 12.3.12, \(f^{(n)}(x)=(-1)^{n+1}\frac{(n-1)!}{(1+x)^n}\). So \(f(0)=0\), \(f'(0)=1\), \(f''(0)=-1\) and \(f'''(0)=2\), and \[T_3(f,0)(x)=x-\frac{x^2}2+\frac{x^3}3.\] With \(x=0.2\), \[\ln1.2\approx T_3(f,0)(0.2)=0.2-0.02+\frac{0.008}3=\frac{137}{750}=0.18266\ldots.\] By Lagrange’s Form of the Remainder (Theorem 12.3.7), there is a \(\xi\in(0,0.2)\) with \[R_3(0.2)=\frac{f^{(4)}(\xi)}{4!}(0.2)^4=\frac{-6}{24(1+\xi)^4}(0.2)^4=-\frac{(0.2)^4}{4(1+\xi)^4}.\] Since \((1+\xi)^4>1\), \[|R_3(0.2)|<\frac{(0.2)^4}{4}=\frac{0.0016}{4}=0.0004<0.0005.\] So \(\ln1.2\approx0.18267\) with an error less than \(0.0004\), which is less than \(0.0005\). So the estimate is accurate to three decimal places. Since \(R_3(0.2)<0\), the estimate is a little too large. In fact \(0.18226<\ln1.2<0.18267\).

Exercise 12.3.4. Hint. By Theorem 12.3.7, \(|R_n(x)|\le e^{|x|}\frac{|x|^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}\). Use Exercise 10.2.5.

Solution. Let \(f(x)=e^x\). Since \(\frac{d}{dx}e^x=e^x\) (Theorem 9.4.4), \(f^{(n)}(x)=e^x\) for all \(n\). So \(f\in C^\infty(\mathbb{R})\) and \(f^{(k)}(0)=e^0=1\) for all \(k\). So \(T(e^x,0)=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{x^k}{k!}\).

Let \(x\in\mathbb{R}\). If \(x=0\), both sides equal \(1\). Suppose \(x\ne0\). By Lagrange’s Form of the Remainder (Theorem 12.3.7) on \(I=\mathbb{R}\), for each \(n\) there is a \(\xi\) between \(0\) and \(x\) with \[R_n(x)=\frac{e^\xi}{(n+1)!}x^{n+1}.\] Since \(\xi\le|x|\) and \(E\) is increasing, \(e^\xi\le e^{|x|}\). So \[|R_n(x)|\le e^{|x|}\frac{|x|^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}.\] By Exercise 10.2.5 with \(p=|x|\), \(\frac{|x|^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}\to0\). So \(R_n(x)\to0\). By Theorem 12.3.6, \(e^x=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{x^k}{k!}\) for all \(x\in\mathbb{R}\).

Exercise 12.4.2. The proof of Theorem 12.4.12 (4) showed \(\sin\frac\pi4=\cos\frac\pi4>0\). Since their squares add to \(1\), \(2\sin^2\frac\pi4=1\). So \(\sin\frac\pi4=\cos\frac\pi4=\frac1{\sqrt2}=\frac{\sqrt2}2\).

Let \(s=\sin\frac\pi6\). Then \(s>0\) by Theorem 12.4.10 (5). By Theorem 12.4.5 and Corollary 12.4.6 (1), \[\sin3x=\sin2x\cos x+\cos2x\sin x=2\sin x(1-\sin^2x)+(1-2\sin^2x)\sin x=3\sin x-4\sin^3x.\] With \(x=\frac\pi6\), this gives \(1=\sin\frac\pi2=3s-4s^3\). So \[0=4s^3-3s+1=(s+1)(2s-1)^2.\] Since \(s>0\), \(s=\frac12\). Also \(\cos\frac\pi6>0\) by Theorem 12.4.8, and \(\cos^2\frac\pi6=1-\frac14=\frac34\). So \(\cos\frac\pi6=\frac{\sqrt3}2\).

Exercise 12.4.3. Hint. Divide the numerator and denominator of \(\frac{\sin(x+y)}{\cos(x+y)}\) by \(\cos x\cos y\).

Exercise 12.4.4. By Theorem 12.4.8, \(\cos x>0\) for \(0\le x<\frac\pi2\), and \(\cos\) is even. So \(\cos x>0\) for \(|x|<\frac\pi2\). Let \(-\frac\pi2\le a<b\le\frac\pi2\). By Theorem 7.2.4, \(\sin b-\sin a=\cos\xi\,(b-a)\) for some \(\xi\in(a,b)\). Since \(|\xi|<\frac\pi2\), this is positive. So \(\sin\) is strictly increasing on \(\left[-\frac\pi2,\frac\pi2\right]\). Since \(\sin\left(\pm\frac\pi2\right)=\pm1\), Theorem 6.3.10 shows that \(\sin\) maps \(\left[-\frac\pi2,\frac\pi2\right]\) onto \([-1,1]\).

Apply Theorem 7.2.9 to \(\sin\) on \(I=\left(-\frac\pi2,\frac\pi2\right)\), where \(\sin'=\cos\ne0\) and \(\sin[I]=(-1,1)\). If \(y=\sin x\) with \(|x|<\frac\pi2\), then \(\cos x>0\), so \(\cos x=\sqrt{1-\sin^2x}=\sqrt{1-y^2}\). Thus \[\arcsin'(y)=\frac1{\cos x}=\frac1{\sqrt{1-y^2}}.\]

Let \(y\in[-1,1]\) and \(x=\arcsin y\). Then \(\frac\pi2-x\in[0,\pi]\), and by Theorems 12.4.5 and 12.4.10, \[\cos\left(\tfrac\pi2-x\right)=\cos\tfrac\pi2\cos x+\sin\tfrac\pi2\sin x=\sin x=y.\] Since \(\arccos\) is the inverse of \(\cos\) on \([0,\pi]\), \(\arccos y=\frac\pi2-x\). So \(\arcsin y+\arccos y=\frac\pi2\).

Exercise 12.4.5. Let \(u(t)=y\left(\frac t\omega\right)\). By the Chain Rule, \(u'(t)=\frac1\omega y'\left(\frac t\omega\right)\) and \[u''(t)=\frac1{\omega^2}y''\left(\frac t\omega\right)=-y\left(\frac t\omega\right)=-u(t).\] So \(u''+u=0\), with \(u(0)=y(0)\) and \(u'(0)=\frac{y'(0)}\omega\). By Theorem 12.4.4, \(u(t)=y(0)\cos t+\frac{y'(0)}\omega\sin t\). Then \(y(x)=u(\omega x)=y(0)\cos\omega x+\frac{y'(0)}\omega\sin\omega x\).

Exercise 12.4.7. Existence. On \((0,\pi)\), \(\cos'=-\sin<0\) by Theorem 12.4.10 (5). So \(\cos\) is strictly decreasing on \([0,\pi]\), by Theorem 7.2.4 as in Exercise 12.4.4. Since \(\cos0=1\), \(\cos\pi=-1\) and \(|a|\le1\), Theorem 6.3.10 gives an \(s\in[0,\pi]\) with \(\cos s=a\). Then \(\sin s\ge0\) and \(\sin^2s=1-a^2=b^2\), so \(\sin s=|b|\). If \(b\ge0\), let \(t=s\). If \(b<0\), then \(\sin s>0\), so \(0<s<\pi\). Let \(t=2\pi-s\in(\pi,2\pi)\). By Theorem 12.4.10 (4) and Theorem 12.4.2, \(\cos t=\cos(-s)=a\) and \(\sin t=\sin(-s)=-|b|=b\).

Uniqueness. Suppose \(t_1,t_2\in[0,2\pi)\) both work. By Theorem 12.4.5 and Theorem 12.4.2, \[\cos(t_1-t_2)=\cos t_1\cos t_2+\sin t_1\sin t_2=a^2+b^2=1\] and \(\sin(t_1-t_2)=\sin t_1\cos t_2-\cos t_1\sin t_2=ba-ab=0\). By Theorem 12.4.10 (6), \(t_1-t_2=k\pi\) for some \(k\in\mathbb{Z}\). By Theorem 12.4.10 (3), \(\cos(k\pi)=(-1)^k\). So \(k\) is even. Since \(|t_1-t_2|<2\pi\), \(k=0\) and \(t_1=t_2\).

Exercise 12.5.1. Hint. For \(x\ge0\) every term of the series is at least \(0\). Then \(E(x)\ge\frac{x^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}\).

Exercise 12.5.2. By Definition 12.5.3, \(e=E(1)=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac1{k!}\). So \(e-\sum_{k=0}^n\frac1{k!}=\sum_{k=n+1}^\infty\frac1{k!}>0\). For \(k\ge n+1\), \(\frac1{k!}\le\frac1{(n+1)!}\cdot\frac1{(n+1)^{k-n-1}}\), with strict inequality for \(k\ge n+2\). So by the geometric series, \[\sum_{k=n+1}^\infty\frac1{k!}<\frac1{(n+1)!}\sum_{j=0}^\infty\frac1{(n+1)^j}=\frac1{(n+1)!}\cdot\frac{n+1}n=\frac1{n!\,n}.\] With \(n=7\), \(\sum_{k=0}^7\frac1{k!}=\frac{685}{252}=2.71825\ldots\) and \(\frac1{7!\cdot7}=\frac1{35280}<0.00003\). So \(2.718<e<2.71826+0.00003<2.719\).

Exercise 12.5.3. Suppose \(e=\frac pq\) with \(p,q\in\mathbb{N}\). Let \[a=q!\left(e-\sum_{k=0}^q\frac1{k!}\right)=(q-1)!\,p-\sum_{k=0}^q\frac{q!}{k!}.\] Each \(\frac{q!}{k!}\) with \(k\le q\) is an integer, so \(a\in\mathbb{Z}\). By Exercise 12.5.2 with \(n=q\), \(0<a<\frac{q!}{q!\,q}=\frac1q\le1\). No integer lies strictly between \(0\) and \(1\). So \(e\) is irrational.
