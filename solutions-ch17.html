Solutions for Chapter 17. Differentiation and Integration

Exercise 17.1.1. The union of the two intervals is \([0,1]\), so \(\mathcal{I}\) covers \(E\). The two intervals overlap on \(\left[\frac13,\frac23\right]\). So a disjoint subcollection has at most one of them. If it is \(\left[0,\frac23\right]\), it misses \(\left(\frac23,1\right]\). If it is \(\left[\frac13,1\right]\), it misses \(\left[0,\frac13\right)\). If it is empty, it misses all of \([0,1]\). In every case the part of \(E\) that is missed has measure at least \(\frac13\).

Here \(m^*(E)=1<\infty\). The hypothesis that fails is that \(\mathcal{I}\) covers \(E\) in the sense of Vitali. For example, the only interval of \(\mathcal{I}\) that contains \(0\) has length \(\frac23\). So for \(\varepsilon\le\frac23\), no interval of \(\mathcal{I}\) contains \(0\) and has length less than \(\varepsilon\).

Exercise 17.2.1. Hint. Suppose \(f(c)>f(d)\) with \(c<d\). Choose \(\varepsilon>0\) so that \(g(x)=f(x)+\varepsilon x\) still has \(g(c)>g(d)\), and choose \(r\) with \(g(d)<r<g(c)\). Look at the largest \(x\in[c,d]\) with \(g(x)\ge r\).

Solution. Two facts are used. First, for any \(c\), the difference quotients of \(g(x)=f(x)+cx\) are those of \(f\) plus \(c\). So each Dini derivative of \(g\) is the matching Dini derivative of \(f\) plus \(c\). Second, it is enough to show \(f(c)\le f(d)\) whenever \(a<c<d<b\). Indeed, if \(a\le x<y\le b\), choose \(c_k\to x\) and \(d_k\to y\) with \(a<c_k<d_k<b\). Then \(f(c_k)\le f(d_k)\), and \(f(x)\le f(y)\) by continuity.

The case \(D_+f\ge0\). Suppose \(a<c<d<b\) and \(f(c)>f(d)\). Choose \(\varepsilon>0\) with \(\varepsilon(d-c)<f(c)-f(d)\), and let \(g(x)=f(x)+\varepsilon x\). Then \(g(c)>g(d)\). Choose \(r\) with \(g(d)<r<g(c)\). Let \(x_0=\sup\{x\in[c,d]:g(x)\ge r\}\). Since \(g\) is continuous, \(g(x_0)\ge r\). (Take points of this set that tend to \(x_0\).) So \(x_0\ne d\), and \(c\le x_0<d\). For \(x_0<x\le d\), \(g(x)<r\le g(x_0)\). So every difference quotient \(\frac{g(x_0+h)-g(x_0)}{h}\) with \(0<h\le d-x_0\) is negative. So \(D^+g(x_0)\le0\), and then \(D_+g(x_0)\le0\). But \(D_+g(x_0)=D_+f(x_0)+\varepsilon\ge\varepsilon>0\), since \(x_0\in(a,b)\). This contradiction shows \(f(c)\le f(d)\).

The case \(D^-f\ge0\). Suppose \(a<c<d<b\) and \(f(c)>f(d)\). Define \(\varepsilon\), \(g\) and \(r\) as before. Let \(x_1=\inf\{x\in[c,d]:g(x)\le r\}\). By continuity, \(g(x_1)\le r\). So \(x_1\ne c\), and \(c<x_1\le d\). For \(c\le x<x_1\), \(g(x)>r\ge g(x_1)\). So every difference quotient \(\frac{g(x_1)-g(x_1-h)}{h}\) with \(0<h\le x_1-c\) is negative, and \(D^-g(x_1)\le0\). But \(D^-g(x_1)=D^-f(x_1)+\varepsilon\ge\varepsilon>0\). This contradiction shows \(f(c)\le f(d)\).

All four derivatives. The first argument showed \(D^+g(x_0)\le0\). So it also proves the theorem when \(D^+f\ge0\). The second proves it when \(D^-f\ge0\). Since \(D_+f\le D^+f\) and \(D_-f\le D^-f\), the hypothesis \(D_+f\ge0\) implies \(D^+f\ge0\), and \(D_-f\ge0\) implies \(D^-f\ge0\). So the cases \(D^+f\ge0\) and \(D^-f\ge0\) cover all four.

Exercise 17.2.4. Hint. Apply Theorem 17.2.3 to \(-f\).

Solution. The function \(-f\) is increasing on \([a,b]\). By Theorem 17.2.3, \(-f\) is differentiable a.e., \((-f)'\) is measurable, and \[\int_{[a,b]}(-f)'\le-f(b)-\bigl(-f(a)\bigr)=f(a)-f(b).\] Wherever \(-f\) is differentiable, so is \(f\), with \(f'=-(-f)'\). So \(f\) is differentiable a.e. Since \((-f)'\ge0\) and its integral is finite, \((-f)'\) is integrable. By Theorem 16.5.4 (1), \(f'\) is integrable and \[\int_{[a,b]}f'=-\int_{[a,b]}(-f)'\ge-\bigl(f(a)-f(b)\bigr)=f(b)-f(a).\]

Exercise 17.3.1. Hint. Add the point \(y\) to a partition of \([a,x]\).

Solution. If \(x=a\), then \(P_f[a,a]=N_f[a,a]=0\) by convention, as in the proof of Theorem 17.3.3, and there is nothing to prove. Suppose \(a<x<y\). Let \(\mathcal{P}=\{x_0,\dots,x_k\}\) be a partition of \([a,x]\). Then \(\mathcal{P}'=\mathcal{P}\cup\{y\}\) is a partition of \([a,y]\), with one more subinterval \([x,y]\). So \[p(\mathcal{P}')=p(\mathcal{P})+[f(y)-f(x)]^+\ge p(\mathcal{P}).\] So \(p(\mathcal{P})\le p(\mathcal{P}')\le P_f[a,y]\). Taking the supremum over all partitions \(\mathcal{P}\) of \([a,x]\) gives \(P_f[a,x]\le P_f[a,y]\). The same argument with \(n(\mathcal{P})\) gives \(N_f[a,x]\le N_f[a,y]\). (These hold in \([0,\infty]\), even if \(f\) is not of bounded variation.)

Exercise 17.3.4. Hint. \(\sin x\) increases on \([0,\frac\pi2]\), decreases on \([\frac\pi2,\frac{3\pi}2]\), and increases on \([\frac{3\pi}2,2\pi]\). Find \(V_f\) with Theorem 8.2.1, then use Exercise 17.3.3.

Exercise 17.3.5. Hint. Let \(V_f(x)=V_f[a,x]\). Show that \(|f'|\le V_f'\) a.e.

Solution. Let \(V_f(x)=V_f[a,x]\), with \(V_f(a)=0\). By Theorem 8.2.2, \(V_f\) is increasing. By Theorem 17.2.3, \(V_f\) is differentiable a.e., and \[\int_{[a,b]}V_f'\le V_f(b)-V_f(a)=V_f[a,b].\] By Corollary 17.3.4, \(f\) is differentiable a.e., and \(f'\) is integrable.

Let \(a\le x<y\le b\). By Remark 8.1.6 and Theorem 8.2.1, \[|f(y)-f(x)|\le V_f[x,y]=V_f(y)-V_f(x).\] Let \(x\in[a,b)\) be a point where both \(f\) and \(V_f\) are differentiable. For \(h>0\) small, divide by \(h\) with \(y=x+h\). Then \(\left|\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\right|\le\frac{V_f(x+h)-V_f(x)}{h}\). Letting \(h\to0^+\) gives \(|f'(x)|\le V_f'(x)\). So \(|f'|\le V_f'\) a.e. By Theorem 16.4.2 (3), \[\int_{[a,b]}|f'|\le\int_{[a,b]}V_f'\le V_f[a,b].\]

Exercise 17.4.2. Hint. Apply Exercise 17.4.1 to \(f=\chi_E\).

Exercise 17.4.3. Hint. \(|F(y)-F(x)|=\left|\int_{[x,y]}f\right|\le\int_{[x,y]}|f|\).

Solution. Since \(f\) is measurable and \(|f|\le K\) on \([a,b]\), \(f\) is integrable, with \(\int_{[a,b]}|f|\le K(b-a)\). Let \(F(x)=F(a)+\int_{[a,x]}f\). Let \(a\le x<y\le b\). Since \([a,y]\) is the disjoint union of \([a,x]\) and \((x,y]\), Theorem 16.5.4 (4) gives \(F(y)-F(x)=\int_{(x,y]}f\). By Exercise 16.5.3 and Theorem 16.3.5 (5), \[|F(y)-F(x)|=\left|\int_{(x,y]}f\right|\le\int_{(x,y]}|f|\le K\,m\bigl((x,y]\bigr)=K(y-x).\] By symmetry, and trivially when \(x=y\), \(|F(x)-F(y)|\le K|x-y|\) for all \(x,y\in[a,b]\).

Exercise 17.4.4. Hint. The indefinite integral \(F\) is continuous, and \(F(c)=F(d)\) for rational \(c<d\). So \(F\) is constant. Then use Lemma 17.4.3.

Solution. Let \(F(x)=\int_{[a,x]}f\). By Lemma 17.4.2, \(F\) is continuous on \([a,b]\). Let \(c<d\) be rational numbers in \([a,b]\). As in the proof of Exercise 17.4.3, \(F(d)-F(c)=\int_{(c,d]}f\). The sets \([c,d]\) and \((c,d]\) differ by one point, which has measure zero, so \(\int_{(c,d]}f=\int_{[c,d]}f=0\). So \(F\) takes the same value at all rational points of \([a,b]\).

Fix a rational \(q_0\in[a,b]\). Every \(x\in[a,b]\) is the limit of a sequence of rational numbers in \([a,b]\), by the density of \(\mathbb{Q}\) (Theorem 1.7.11). Since \(F\) is continuous, \(F(x)=F(q_0)\). So \(F\) is constant on \([a,b]\). Since \(F(a)=0\), \(\int_{[a,x]}f=0\) for every \(x\in[a,b]\). By Lemma 17.4.3, \(f=0\) a.e. on \([a,b]\).

Exercise 17.5.1. Hint. Let \(g(x)=\int_{[a,x]}f'\). Use Theorem 17.2.3 on \([x,y]\) to show \(f-g\) is increasing.

Solution. Let \(f\) be increasing on \([a,b]\). By Theorem 17.2.3, \(f'\) exists a.e., \(f'\ge0\), and \(\int_{[a,b]}f'\le f(b)-f(a)<\infty\). So \(f'\) is integrable. Let \[g(x)=\int_{[a,x]}f'\quad\text{and}\quad h=f-g.\] Then \(g\) is an indefinite integral, so \(g\in AC[a,b]\) by Theorem 17.5.8 (see Exercise 17.5.2).

The function \(h\) is increasing. Let \(a\le x<y\le b\). Then \(g(y)-g(x)=\int_{(x,y]}f'=\int_{[x,y]}f'\), since one point has measure zero. The function \(f\) is increasing on \([x,y]\), so Theorem 17.2.3 on \([x,y]\) gives \(\int_{[x,y]}f'\le f(y)-f(x)\). So \[h(y)-h(x)=\bigl(f(y)-f(x)\bigr)-\int_{[x,y]}f'\ge0.\] By Theorem 17.4.5, \(g'=f'\) a.e. So \(h'=f'-g'=0\) a.e., and \(h\) is singular. Thus \(f=g+h\) is the sum of an absolutely continuous function and a singular function.

Exercise 17.5.2. Hint. Use Theorem 16.4.14.

Solution. Let \(F(x)=F(a)+\int_{[a,x]}f\), where \(f\) is integrable on \([a,b]\). Let \(\varepsilon>0\). By Theorem 16.4.14, applied to \(|f|\), there is a \(\delta>0\) such that \(\int_A|f|<\varepsilon\) for every measurable \(A\subset[a,b]\) with \(m(A)<\delta\).

Let \(\{(x_i,x_i')\}_{i=1}^n\) be nonoverlapping subintervals of \([a,b]\) with \(x_i<x_i'\) and \(\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i'-x_i)<\delta\). As in Exercise 17.4.3, \(F(x_i')-F(x_i)=\int_{(x_i,x_i']}f\). The half-open intervals \((x_i,x_i']\) are pairwise disjoint, since the open intervals are. Let \(A\) be their union. Then \(m(A)=\sum_i(x_i'-x_i)<\delta\). By Exercise 16.5.3 and Theorem 16.4.10, \[\sum_{i=1}^n|F(x_i')-F(x_i)|\le\sum_{i=1}^n\int_{(x_i,x_i']}|f|=\int_A|f|<\varepsilon.\] So \(F\) is absolutely continuous on \([a,b]\).

Exercise 17.5.3. Hint. Continuous functions on \([a,b]\) are bounded. For \(fg\), use \(|f(y)g(y)-f(x)g(x)|\le|f(y)||g(y)-g(x)|+|g(x)||f(y)-f(x)|\).

Solution. Let \(\{(x_i,x_i')\}\) denote a finite collection of nonoverlapping subintervals of \([a,b]\), and let \(\varepsilon>0\).

The sum. Choose \(\delta_1\) for \(f\) and \(\delta_2\) for \(g\) in Definition 17.5.1, both with \(\frac\varepsilon 2\), and let \(\delta=\min\{\delta_1,\delta_2\}\). If \(\sum|x_i'-x_i|<\delta\), then by the Triangle Inequality \[\sum|(f+g)(x_i')-(f+g)(x_i)|\le\sum|f(x_i')-f(x_i)|+\sum|g(x_i')-g(x_i)|<\frac\varepsilon 2+\frac\varepsilon 2=\varepsilon.\]

The multiple. If \(c=0\), then \(cf=0\) is constant. Otherwise choose \(\delta\) for \(f\) with \(\frac{\varepsilon}{|c|}\). Then \(\sum|cf(x_i')-cf(x_i)|=|c|\sum|f(x_i')-f(x_i)|<\varepsilon\).

The product. An absolutely continuous function is continuous, so \(f\) and \(g\) are bounded on \([a,b]\) (Theorem 6.3.14). Choose \(K>0\) with \(|f|\le K\) and \(|g|\le K\). For \(x,y\in[a,b]\), \[|f(y)g(y)-f(x)g(x)|\le|f(y)||g(y)-g(x)|+|g(x)||f(y)-f(x)|\le K\bigl(|g(y)-g(x)|+|f(y)-f(x)|\bigr).\] Choose \(\delta\) for both \(f\) and \(g\) with \(\frac{\varepsilon}{2K}\). If \(\sum|x_i'-x_i|<\delta\), then \[\sum|fg(x_i')-fg(x_i)|\le K\left(\sum|g(x_i')-g(x_i)|+\sum|f(x_i')-f(x_i)|\right)<K\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2K}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2K}\right)=\varepsilon.\] So \(f+g\), \(cf\) and \(fg\) are in \(AC[a,b]\).

Exercise 17.6.1. Hint. Prove versions of both identities for the functions \(\phi_n\), using the symmetry of \(C_n\), and let \(n\to\infty\). For the integral, \(\phi\) is continuous. Use Theorem 16.3.3 and the substitution \(x\mapsto1-x\).

Solution. By Lemma 5.1.2 (2), \(C_n\) is the union of the intervals \(\left[s,s+3^{-n}\right]\), where \(s=\sum_{k=1}^n\frac{d_k}{3^k}\) with each \(d_k\in\{0,2\}\). Also \(\phi_n(x)=\left(\frac32\right)^nm\bigl(C_n\cap[0,x]\bigr)\), by Definition 16.2.1.

Symmetry. The reflection \(t\mapsto1-t\) maps \(\left[s,s+3^{-n}\right]\) onto \(\left[1-s-3^{-n},1-s\right]\). Since \(\sum_{k=1}^n\frac2{3^k}=1-3^{-n}\), \[1-s-3^{-n}=\sum_{k=1}^n\frac{2-d_k}{3^k},\] and each \(2-d_k\in\{0,2\}\). So the reflection maps \(C_n\) onto itself. It maps \(C_n\cap[0,1-x]\) onto \(C_n\cap[x,1]\). Both are finite unions of intervals, and the reflection does not change lengths. So \[\phi_n(1-x)=\left(\tfrac32\right)^nm\bigl(C_n\cap[x,1]\bigr)=\left(\tfrac32\right)^n\Bigl(m(C_n)-m\bigl(C_n\cap[0,x]\bigr)\Bigr)=1-\phi_n(x),\] using \(m(C_n)=\left(\frac23\right)^n\) and \(m(\{x\})=0\). Letting \(n\to\infty\) gives \(\phi(1-x)=1-\phi(x)\).

Scaling. The map \(t\mapsto\frac t3\) sends \(\left[s,s+3^{-n}\right]\) to \(\left[\frac s3,\frac s3+3^{-(n+1)}\right]\), and \(\frac s3=\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\frac{d_k'}{3^k}\) with \(d_1'=0\) and \(d_{k+1}'=d_k\). These are exactly the intervals of \(C_{n+1}\) whose first digit is \(0\), which are the intervals of \(C_{n+1}\) inside \(\left[0,\frac13\right]\). So \(\frac13C_n=C_{n+1}\cap\left[0,\frac13\right]\). For \(x\in[0,1]\), this gives \(C_{n+1}\cap\left[0,\frac x3\right]=\frac13\bigl(C_n\cap[0,x]\bigr)\). Scaling by \(\frac13\) multiplies the lengths of intervals by \(\frac13\). So \[\phi_{n+1}\left(\tfrac x3\right)=\left(\tfrac32\right)^{n+1}\cdot\tfrac13\,m\bigl(C_n\cap[0,x]\bigr)=\tfrac12\phi_n(x).\] Letting \(n\to\infty\) gives \(\phi\left(\frac x3\right)=\frac12\phi(x)\).

The integral. Since \(\phi\) is continuous, \(\int_{[0,1]}\phi=\int_0^1\phi\), by Theorem 16.3.3. The substitution \(x=1-t\) (Theorem 9.5.3) gives \(\int_0^1\phi(1-t)\,dt=\int_0^1\phi(x)\,dx\). So, by the symmetry, \[\int_0^1\phi=\int_0^1\phi(1-t)\,dt=\int_0^1\bigl(1-\phi(t)\bigr)\,dt=1-\int_0^1\phi.\] So \(\int_{[0,1]}\phi=\frac12\).
