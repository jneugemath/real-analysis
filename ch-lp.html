Chapter 16 defined \(L^1(E)\), the functions whose absolute value has a finite integral. This chapter replaces \(|f|\) by \(|f|^p\). The resulting spaces \(L^p(E)\) are where much of modern analysis takes place, including Fourier analysis, probability and the study of differential equations. Section 18.1 defines them and proves the inequalities of Hölder and Minkowski. Section 18.2 proves that \(L^p\) is complete, the analogue of the completeness of \(\mathbb{R}\). Section 18.3 shows that step functions and continuous functions are dense in \(L^p[a,b]\).

The whole chapter is marked with a star. It can be skipped. Nothing else in the book depends on it. It uses Chapters 15 and 16, and the power functions \(x^p\) for real \(p\) (see Section 9.4).

Throughout, \(E\) is a measurable subset of \(\mathbb{R}\). Two functions that are equal almost everywhere on \(E\) have the same integrals over \(E\). So in this chapter they are treated as the same function. This is what makes \(\left\lVert f\right\rVert_p=0\) mean \(f=0\).

18.1 The Spaces \(L^p\)

Definition 18.1.1. Let \(1\le p<\infty\). A measurable function \(f\) on \(E\) is in \(L^p(E)\) if \(\int_E|f|^p<\infty\). For such \(f\), the \(L^p\) norm of \(f\) is \[\left\lVert f\right\rVert_p=\left(\int_E|f|^p\right)^{1/p}.\] A measurable function \(f\) is in \(L^\infty(E)\) if there is an \(M\) with \(|f|\le M\) almost everywhere on \(E\). Then \(\left\lVert f\right\rVert_\infty\) is the infimum of all such \(M\). Write \(L^p[a,b]\) for \(L^p([a,b])\).

For \(p=1\) this is the space \(L^1(E)\) of Definition 16.5.3. The infimum in the definition of \(\left\lVert f\right\rVert_\infty\) is attained, so \(|f|\le\left\lVert f\right\rVert_\infty\) almost everywhere (Exercise 18.1.4). The number \(q\) with \(\frac1p+\frac1q=1\) is called the conjugate exponent of \(p\). For \(p=1\), take \(q=\infty\), and for \(p=2\), \(q=2\).

Lemma 18.1.2 (Young’s Inequality).

Let \(1<p<\infty\), let \(\frac1p+\frac1q=1\), and let \(a,b\ge0\). Then \(ab\le\frac{a^p}p+\frac{b^q}q\).

Proof.

If \(b=0\), there is nothing to prove. Fix \(b>0\), and let \(h(a)=\frac{a^p}p+\frac{b^q}q-ab\) for \(a\ge0\). Then \(h'(a)=a^{p-1}-b\), which is negative for \(a<a_0=b^{1/(p-1)}\) and positive for \(a>a_0\). So \(h\) has its smallest value at \(a_0\), by Theorem 7.2.7. Since \(\frac1{p-1}=\frac qp\), \(a_0^p=b^q\) and \(a_0b=b^{q/p+1}=b^q\), because \(\frac qp+1=q\left(\frac1p+\frac1q\right)=q\). So \(h(a_0)=\frac{b^q}p+\frac{b^q}q-b^q=0\), and \(h\ge0\).

∎

Theorem 18.1.3 (Hölder’s Inequality).

Let \(1\le p\le\infty\) and let \(q\) be its conjugate exponent. If \(f\in L^p(E)\) and \(g\in L^q(E)\), then \(fg\in L^1(E)\) and \[\int_E|fg|\le\left\lVert f\right\rVert_p\left\lVert g\right\rVert_q.\]

Proof.

If \(p=1\), then \(|fg|\le|f|\left\lVert g\right\rVert_\infty\) almost everywhere, and integrating gives the result. The case \(p=\infty\) is the same with \(f\) and \(g\) exchanged. Let \(1<p<\infty\). If \(\left\lVert f\right\rVert_p=0\), then \(f=0\) almost everywhere, and both sides are \(0\). The same holds if \(\left\lVert g\right\rVert_q=0\). Otherwise, let \(F=\frac{|f|}{\left\lVert f\right\rVert_p}\) and \(G=\frac{|g|}{\left\lVert g\right\rVert_q}\). Then \(\int_EF^p=\int_EG^q=1\). By Lemma 18.1.2, \(FG\le\frac{F^p}p+\frac{G^q}q\). Integrating, \(\int_EFG\le\frac1p+\frac1q=1\). Multiply by \(\left\lVert f\right\rVert_p\left\lVert g\right\rVert_q\).

∎

Theorem 18.1.4 (Minkowski’s Inequality).

Let \(1\le p\le\infty\). If \(f,g\in L^p(E)\), then \(f+g\in L^p(E)\) and \(\left\lVert f+g\right\rVert_p\le\left\lVert f\right\rVert_p+\left\lVert g\right\rVert_p\).

Proof.

The cases \(p=1\) and \(p=\infty\) follow from \(|f+g|\le|f|+|g|\) (Exercise 18.1.4). Let \(1<p<\infty\). Since \(|f+g|\le2\max\{|f|,|g|\}\), \(|f+g|^p\le2^p(|f|^p+|g|^p)\). So \(f+g\in L^p(E)\). If \(\left\lVert f+g\right\rVert_p=0\) there is nothing to prove. Let \(q\) be the conjugate exponent, so \((p-1)q=p\). Then \(|f+g|^{p-1}\in L^q(E)\), with \(\left\lVert |f+g|^{p-1}\right\rVert_q=\left\lVert f+g\right\rVert_p^{p/q}\). By Theorem 18.1.3, \[\int_E|f+g|^p\le\int_E|f||f+g|^{p-1}+\int_E|g||f+g|^{p-1}\le\bigl(\left\lVert f\right\rVert_p+\left\lVert g\right\rVert_p\bigr)\left\lVert f+g\right\rVert_p^{p/q}.\] Divide by \(\left\lVert f+g\right\rVert_p^{p/q}\). Since \(p-\frac pq=1\), this gives \(\left\lVert f+g\right\rVert_p\le\left\lVert f\right\rVert_p+\left\lVert g\right\rVert_p\).

∎

So \(L^p(E)\) is closed under addition, and clearly under multiplication by constants. It is a vector space, and \(\left\lVert \cdot\right\rVert_p\) behaves like a length. It satisfies \(\left\lVert cf\right\rVert_p=|c|\left\lVert f\right\rVert_p\) and the triangle inequality, and \(\left\lVert f\right\rVert_p=0\) only when \(f=0\) almost everywhere. The distance between \(f\) and \(g\) is \(\left\lVert f-g\right\rVert_p\). For \(p=2\), Hölder’s Inequality is the Cauchy–Schwarz Inequality (Exercise 18.1.3).

On a set of finite measure, larger \(p\) means a smaller space (Exercise 18.1.1). On \((0,\infty)\), no space contains another (Exercise 18.1.2).

18.2 Completeness

Definition 18.2.1. Let \(1\le p\le\infty\). A sequence \(\{f_n\}\) in \(L^p(E)\) converges in \(L^p\) to \(f\in L^p(E)\) if \(\left\lVert f_n-f\right\rVert_p\to0\). It is a Cauchy sequence in \(L^p\) if for every \(\varepsilon>0\) there is an \(N\) with \(\left\lVert f_n-f_m\right\rVert_p<\varepsilon\) for all \(m,n\ge N\).

For \(p=\infty\), convergence in \(L^\infty\) is uniform convergence off a set of measure zero. For \(p<\infty\), convergence in \(L^p\) is a different kind of convergence from those in Chapter 15. The sequence \(f_n=n\chi_{(0,1/n)}\) converges to \(0\) at every point, but \(\left\lVert f_n\right\rVert_1=1\) for all \(n\), so it does not converge to \(0\) in \(L^1\). (See Figure 16.2.) In the other direction, a sequence can converge in \(L^p\) without converging at any point (Exercise 18.2.1). The next theorem shows that some subsequence always converges almost everywhere.

Theorem 18.2.2 (Riesz–Fischer Theorem).

Let \(1\le p<\infty\). Every Cauchy sequence in \(L^p(E)\) converges in \(L^p\) to a function in \(L^p(E)\). Also, some subsequence converges to it almost everywhere.

Proof.

Let \(\{f_n\}\) be Cauchy in \(L^p(E)\). Choose \(n_1<n_2<\cdots\) with \(\left\lVert f_n-f_m\right\rVert_p<2^{-k}\) for all \(m,n\ge n_k\). Then \(\left\lVert f_{n_{k+1}}-f_{n_k}\right\rVert_p<2^{-k}\). Let \[g_K=|f_{n_1}|+\sum_{k=1}^K|f_{n_{k+1}}-f_{n_k}|\quad\text{and}\quad g=\lim_{K\to\infty}g_K.\] By Theorem 18.1.4, \(\left\lVert g_K\right\rVert_p\le\left\lVert f_{n_1}\right\rVert_p+1\) for every \(K\). The functions \(g_K^p\) increase to \(g^p\), so by Theorem 16.4.7, \(\int_Eg^p\le(\left\lVert f_{n_1}\right\rVert_p+1)^p<\infty\). In particular, \(g<\infty\) almost everywhere.

Where \(g(x)<\infty\), the series \(f_{n_1}(x)+\sum_k\bigl(f_{n_{k+1}}(x)-f_{n_k}(x)\bigr)\) converges absolutely. Its partial sums are \(f_{n_{K+1}}(x)\). So \(f_{n_k}(x)\) converges to a number \(f(x)\) almost everywhere. Set \(f=0\) elsewhere. Then \(f\) is measurable, and \(|f|\le g\), so \(f\in L^p(E)\). Also \(|f-f_{n_k}|^p\le(2g)^p\), which is integrable, and \(|f-f_{n_k}|^p\to0\) almost everywhere. By Theorem 16.5.6, \(\left\lVert f-f_{n_k}\right\rVert_p\to0\).

Finally, let \(\varepsilon>0\). Choose \(N\) with \(\left\lVert f_n-f_m\right\rVert_p<\varepsilon\) for \(m,n\ge N\), and choose \(k\) with \(n_k\ge N\) and \(\left\lVert f-f_{n_k}\right\rVert_p<\varepsilon\). For \(n\ge N\), \(\left\lVert f-f_n\right\rVert_p\le\left\lVert f-f_{n_k}\right\rVert_p+\left\lVert f_{n_k}-f_n\right\rVert_p<2\varepsilon\). So \(f_n\to f\) in \(L^p\).

∎

In the language of later courses, \(L^p(E)\) is a Banach space, a complete normed vector space. The theorem is also true for \(p=\infty\) (Exercise 18.2.2). For the Riemann integral, nothing like it holds. A sequence of Riemann integrable functions can be Cauchy for \(\int|f_n-f_m|\) without converging to any Riemann integrable function. This is one of the main reasons the Lebesgue integral replaced the Riemann integral.

18.3 Approximation in \(L^p\)

Theorem 18.3.1. Let \(1\le p<\infty\) and \(f\in L^p[a,b]\). For every \(\varepsilon>0\), there is a step function \(g\) on \([a,b]\) with \(\left\lVert f-g\right\rVert_p<\varepsilon\).

Proof.

Step 1. Cut \(f\) off. For \(N\in\mathbb{N}\), let \(f_N=f\) where \(|f|\le N\), \(f_N=N\) where \(f>N\), and \(f_N=-N\) where \(f<-N\). By Exercise 16.4.5, \(m(\{|f|^p\ge N^p\})\le N^{-p}\int|f|^p\to0\), so \(|f|<\infty\) almost everywhere. So \(f_N\to f\) almost everywhere. Also \(|f-f_N|^p\le|f|^p\), which is integrable. By Theorem 16.5.6, \(\left\lVert f-f_N\right\rVert_p\to0\). Choose \(N\) with \(\left\lVert f-f_N\right\rVert_p<\frac\varepsilon 2\).

Step 2. Approximate the bounded function \(f_N\). Let \(\eta>0\). By Theorem 15.2.7, there is a step function \(g\) with \(-N\le g\le N\) and \(m(A)<\eta\), where \(A=\{x:|f_N(x)-g(x)|\ge\eta\}\). Off \(A\), \(|f_N-g|<\eta\). On \(A\), \(|f_N-g|\le2N\). So \[\int_{[a,b]}|f_N-g|^p\le\eta^p(b-a)+(2N)^p\eta.\] Choose \(\eta\) so small that this is less than \(\left(\frac\varepsilon 2\right)^p\). Then \(\left\lVert f_N-g\right\rVert_p<\frac\varepsilon 2\), and \(\left\lVert f-g\right\rVert_p<\varepsilon\) by Theorem 18.1.4.

∎

The same proof, with the continuous function \(h\) of Theorem 15.2.7 in place of \(g\), shows that continuous functions are dense too (Exercise 18.3.1). So every function in \(L^p[a,b]\), however wild, is close in the \(L^p\) norm to a continuous one. Together with Theorem 18.2.2, this says \(L^p[a,b]\) is exactly what is obtained by filling in the gaps in the continuous functions under the norm \(\left\lVert \cdot\right\rVert_p\), much as \(\mathbb{R}\) fills in the gaps in \(\mathbb{Q}\) in Appendix B.

Exercises

Exercise 18.3.1. Let \(1\le p<\infty\) and \(f\in L^p[a,b]\). Prove that for every \(\varepsilon>0\) there is a continuous function \(h\) on \([a,b]\) with \(\left\lVert f-h\right\rVert_p<\varepsilon\). [Solution]

Exercise 18.3.2. (Riemann–Lebesgue Lemma) Let \(f\in L^1[a,b]\). Prove that \(\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{[a,b]}f(x)\sin nx\,dx=0\). (First take \(f\) to be a step function.) [Hint]

Notes

Frigyes Riesz introduced the spaces \(L^p\) in 1910. Riesz and Ernst Fischer each proved the completeness of \(L^2\) in 1907, which is why Theorem 18.2.2 carries both names. Leonard James Rogers proved the inequality now named for Otto Hölder in 1888, and Hölder found it again in 1889. Hermann Minkowski proved his inequality for sums in 1896. Stefan Banach made complete normed spaces the subject of a general theory in the 1920s. The Riemann–Lebesgue Lemma of Exercise 18.3.2 goes back to Riemann’s 1854 paper on trigonometric series, and Lebesgue proved it for his integral.
