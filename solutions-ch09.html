Solutions for Chapter 9. Integrals

Exercise 9.1.2. Hint. Each \(m_i\) and \(M_i\) lies in \([m,M]\), and \(\sum\Delta x_i=b-a\).

Solution. Let \(\mathcal{P}=\{x_0,\dots,x_n\}\) be a partition of \([a,b]\), and let \(1\le i\le n\). Since \(m\le f(x)\) for all \(x\in[x_{i-1},x_i]\), \(m\) is a lower bound of \(\{f(x):x_{i-1}\le x\le x_i\}\). So \(m\le m_i\). In the same way, \(M_i\le M\). Also \(m_i\le f(x_i)\le M_i\). So \[m\le m_i\le M_i\le M.\] Multiply by \(\Delta x_i>0\) and add over \(i\). Since \(\sum_{i=1}^n\Delta x_i=b-a\), \[m(b-a)=\sum_{i=1}^nm\,\Delta x_i\le\sum_{i=1}^nm_i\Delta x_i\le\sum_{i=1}^nM_i\Delta x_i\le\sum_{i=1}^nM\,\Delta x_i=M(b-a).\] The middle two sums are \(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P},f)\) and \(\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{P},f)\).

Exercise 9.1.5. Hint. Use the regular partition into \(n\) subintervals and \(\sum_{i=1}^ni=\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\).

Solution. Let \(f(x)=2x+1\). For \(n\in\mathbb{N}\), let \(\mathcal{P}_n=\left\{0,\frac1n,\frac2n,\dots,1\right\}\). Since \(f\) is increasing, its infimum and supremum on \(\left[\frac{i-1}{n},\frac in\right]\) are the values at the endpoints. So \[m_i=\frac{2(i-1)}{n}+1\quad\text{and}\quad M_i=\frac{2i}{n}+1.\] Since \(\Delta x_i=\frac1n\) and \(\sum_{i=1}^ni=\frac{n(n+1)}2\), \[\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P}_n,f)=\frac{2}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n(i-1)+1=\frac{n-1}{n}+1=2-\frac1n\] and \[\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{P}_n,f)=\frac{2}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^ni+1=\frac{n+1}{n}+1=2+\frac1n.\] By Definition 9.1.4 and Theorem 9.1.6, for every \(n\in\mathbb{N}\), \[2-\frac1n=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P}_n,f)\le\underline{\int_{0}^{1}}f\le\overline{\int_{0}^{1}}f\le\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{P}_n,f)=2+\frac1n.\] Letting \(n\to\infty\) gives \(\underline{\int_{0}^{1}}f=\overline{\int_{0}^{1}}f=2\). So \(\int_0^1(2x+1)\,dx=2\).

Exercise 9.2.1. Hint. For \(c\ge0\), \(\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{P},cf)=c\,\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{P},f)\) and \(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P},cf)=c\,\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P},f)\). For \(c<0\), multiplying by \(c\) swaps sup and inf. So \(\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{P},cf)=c\,\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P},f)\).

Solution. If \(c=0\), then \(cf\) is the zero function. Every upper and lower sum of \(cf\) is \(0\). So \(cf\in\mathcal{R}[a,b]\) and \(\int_a^bcf=0=c\int_a^bf\).

Suppose \(c\ne0\), and let \(\mathcal{P}=\{x_0,\dots,x_n\}\) be a partition of \([a,b]\). Write \(m_i\) and \(M_i\) for the infimum and supremum of \(f\) on \([x_{i-1},x_i]\).

Suppose \(c>0\). For \(x\in[x_{i-1},x_i]\), \(cm_i\le cf(x)\le cM_i\). So the supremum of \(cf\) on \([x_{i-1},x_i]\) is at most \(cM_i\), and its infimum is at least \(cm_i\). Adding over \(i\), \[c\,\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P},f)\le\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P},cf)\le\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{P},cf)\le c\,\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{P},f).\] Suppose \(c<0\). Multiplying by \(c\) reverses inequalities, so \(cM_i\le cf(x)\le cm_i\). In the same way, \[c\,\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{P},f)\le\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P},cf)\le\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{P},cf)\le c\,\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P},f).\] In both cases, call the outer numbers \(A_\mathcal{P}\le B_\mathcal{P}\). Then \(B_\mathcal{P}-A_\mathcal{P}=|c|\bigl(\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{P},f)-\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P},f)\bigr)\). Since \(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P},f)\le\int_a^bf\le\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{P},f)\), also \(A_\mathcal{P}\le c\int_a^bf\le B_\mathcal{P}\).

Let \(\varepsilon>0\). By Riemann’s Criterion (Theorem 9.2.1), there is a partition \(\mathcal{P}\) with \(\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{P},f)-\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P},f)<\frac{\varepsilon}{|c|}\). Then \(\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{P},cf)-\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P},cf)\le B_\mathcal{P}-A_\mathcal{P}<\varepsilon\). So \(cf\in\mathcal{R}[a,b]\), by Riemann’s Criterion. Both \(\int_a^bcf\) and \(c\int_a^bf\) lie in \([A_\mathcal{P},B_\mathcal{P}]\), an interval of length less than \(\varepsilon\). So they differ by less than \(\varepsilon\). Since \(\varepsilon>0\) was arbitrary, \(\int_a^bcf=c\int_a^bf\).

Exercise 9.2.3. Hint. Show that \(\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{P},f)\le\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{P},g)\) for every partition \(\mathcal{P}\).

Solution. Let \(\mathcal{P}=\{x_0,\dots,x_n\}\) be a partition of \([a,b]\). For \(x\in[x_{i-1},x_i]\), \(f(x)\le g(x)\le\sup\{g(t):x_{i-1}\le t\le x_i\}\). So the supremum of \(f\) on \([x_{i-1},x_i]\) is at most the supremum of \(g\) there. Multiplying by \(\Delta x_i\) and adding gives \(\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{P},f)\le\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{P},g)\). Since \(f\in\mathcal{R}[a,b]\), \[\int_a^bf=\overline{\int_{a}^{b}}f\le\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{P},f)\le\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{P},g).\] So \(\int_a^bf\) is a lower bound of the upper sums of \(g\). Therefore \(\int_a^bf\le\overline{\int_{a}^{b}}g=\int_a^bg\).

Exercise 9.3.2. Hint. If \(|f|\le M\), then \(|F(x)-F(y)|\le M|x-y|\).

Solution. Since \(f\) is bounded, there is an \(M>0\) with \(|f(t)|\le M\) for all \(t\in[a,b]\). By Theorem 9.2.13, \(f\) is integrable on every closed interval in \([a,b]\), and \[F(y)-F(x)=\int_x^yf(t)\,dt\quad\text{for }a\le x<y\le b.\] (If \(x=a\), this is the definition of \(F(y)\), since \(F(a)=0\).) Since \(-M\le f(t)\le M\) on \([x,y]\), Theorem 9.1.3 gives \(-M(y-x)\le\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P},f)\le\int_x^yf\le\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{P},f)\le M(y-x)\) for any partition \(\mathcal{P}\) of \([x,y]\). So \[|F(y)-F(x)|\le M|y-x|\quad\text{for all }x,y\in[a,b].\] Let \(\varepsilon>0\) and \(\delta=\frac\varepsilon M\). If \(x,y\in[a,b]\) and \(|x-y|<\delta\), then \(|F(y)-F(x)|<M\delta=\varepsilon\). So \(F\) is continuous on \([a,b]\). In fact \(F\) is Lipschitz continuous, and so uniformly continuous.

Exercise 9.4.3.

  1. For \(x>0\), \(f(x)=e^{x\ln x}\). By the Chain Rule and the Product Rule, \[f'(x)=e^{x\ln x}\left(\ln x+x\cdot\frac1x\right)=x^x(1+\ln x).\]

  2. Since \(x^x>0\), \(f'(x)\) has the sign of \(1+\ln x\). Since \(\ln\) is strictly increasing and \(\ln\frac1e=-1\), \(f'(x)<0\) for \(0<x<\frac1e\) and \(f'(x)>0\) for \(x>\frac1e\). By Theorem 7.2.7, \(f\) is strictly decreasing on \(\left(0,\frac1e\right]\) and strictly increasing on \(\left[\frac1e,\infty\right)\). So the smallest value of \(f\) is \(f\left(\frac1e\right)=e^{-1/e}\).

  3. Let \(\{x_n\}\) be a sequence with \(x_n>0\) and \(x_n\to0\). By Theorem 9.4.13 (3) with \(\alpha=1\) and Theorem 6.2.8, \(x_n\ln x_n\to0\). Since \(\exp\) is continuous at \(0\), \(x_n^{x_n}=e^{x_n\ln x_n}\to e^0=1\) by Theorem 6.3.9. So \(\lim_{x\to0^+}x^x=1\) by Theorem 6.2.8.

Exercise 9.4.4. Let \(g(x)=f(x)e^{-x}\). By the Chain Rule, \(\frac{d}{dx}e^{-x}=-e^{-x}\). So \[g'(x)=f'(x)e^{-x}-f(x)e^{-x}=0\] for all \(x\in\mathbb{R}\), since \(f'=f\). By Theorem 7.2.7 (5), \(g\) is constant on \(\mathbb{R}\). Since \(g(0)=f(0)=1\), \(f(x)e^{-x}=1\) for all \(x\). Multiplying by \(e^x\) gives \(f(x)=e^x\).

Exercise 9.5.6. For \(-1<x<1\), the Product Rule, the Chain Rule and Theorem 9.5.12 (1) give \[F'(x)=\frac12\left(\sqrt{1-x^2}-\frac{x^2}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}+\frac1{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\right)=\frac12\cdot\frac{(1-x^2)-x^2+1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}=\sqrt{1-x^2}.\] Let \(G(x)=\int_0^x\sqrt{1-t^2}\,dt\) for \(0\le x\le1\). Exercise 9.6.1. Hint. Let \(m\) and \(M\) be the minimum and maximum of \(f\). Then \(m\int_a^bg\le\int_a^bfg\le M\int_a^bg\). If \(\int_a^bg>0\), use the Intermediate Value Theorem. If \(\int_a^bg=0\), any \(c\) works.

Solution. By the Extreme Value Theorem (Theorem 6.3.15), there are \(c_1,c_2\in[a,b]\) with \(m=f(c_1)\le f(x)\le f(c_2)=M\) for all \(x\in[a,b]\). Since \(g(x)\ge0\), \[mg(x)\le f(x)g(x)\le Mg(x)\quad\text{for all }x\in[a,b].\] Since \(f\) is continuous, \(f\in\mathcal{R}[a,b]\), and so \(fg\in\mathcal{R}[a,b]\) by Theorem 9.2.11 (3). By Exercise 9.2.3 and Theorem 9.2.11 (2), \[m\int_a^bg\le\int_a^bfg\le M\int_a^bg.\] Also \(\int_a^bg\ge0\), by Exercise 9.2.3 with the zero function.

If \(\int_a^bg=0\), then \(\int_a^bfg=0\), and every \(c\in[a,b]\) works.

Suppose \(\int_a^bg>0\), and let \(k=\frac{\int_a^bfg}{\int_a^bg}\). Then \(m\le k\le M\). If \(k=m\), take \(c=c_1\). If \(k=M\), take \(c=c_2\). Otherwise \(f(c_1)<k<f(c_2)\). Apply the Intermediate Value Theorem (Theorem 6.3.10) to \(f\) on the closed interval with endpoints \(c_1\) and \(c_2\). It gives a \(c\) between \(c_1\) and \(c_2\), so in \([a,b]\), with \(f(c)=k\). In every case, \(\int_a^bfg=f(c)\int_a^bg\).

Exercise 9.7.1. Hint. For \(p\ne1\), \(\int_a^sx^{-p}\,dx=\frac{s^{1-p}-a^{1-p}}{1-p}\). For \(p=1\), it is \(\ln s-\ln a\). Let \(s\to\infty\). For the second part, integrate over \([t,a]\) and let \(t\to0^+\).

Solution. Two facts about powers are used. If \(q>0\), then \(s^q\to\infty\) as \(s\to\infty\), since \(s^q\) is increasing and \(s^q\ge n\) for \(s\ge n^{1/q}\). So \(s^{-q}=\frac1{s^q}\to0\) as \(s\to\infty\), and \(t^q=\frac{1}{(1/t)^q}\to0\) as \(t\to0^+\). Also \(\ln s\to\infty\) as \(s\to\infty\), since \(\ln\) is increasing and \(\ln(2^n)=n\ln2\) with \(\ln2>0\).

The function \(x^{-p}\) is continuous on \((0,\infty)\), so it is locally integrable there. An antiderivative is \(\frac{x^{1-p}}{1-p}\) if \(p\ne1\), and \(\ln x\) if \(p=1\). By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, for \(0<t<a<s\), \[\int_a^s\frac{dx}{x^p}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{s^{1-p}-a^{1-p}}{1-p}, & p\ne1,\\[1ex] \ln s-\ln a, & p=1,\end{cases} \qquad \int_t^a\frac{dx}{x^p}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{a^{1-p}-t^{1-p}}{1-p}, & p\ne1,\\[1ex] \ln a-\ln t, & p=1.\end{cases}\]

Let \(s\to\infty\). If \(p>1\), then \(s^{1-p}\to0\), and the first integral tends to \(\frac{a^{1-p}}{p-1}\). If \(p<1\), then \(s^{1-p}\to\infty\), and the first integral tends to \(\infty\). If \(p=1\), \(\ln s\to\infty\). So \(\int_a^\infty\frac{dx}{x^p}\) converges if and only if \(p>1\).

Let \(t\to0^+\). If \(p<1\), then \(t^{1-p}\to0\), and the second integral tends to \(\frac{a^{1-p}}{1-p}\). If \(p>1\), then \(t^{1-p}=\frac{1}{t^{p-1}}\to\infty\), and the second integral tends to \(\infty\). If \(p=1\), \(-\ln t=\ln\frac1t\to\infty\). So \(\int_0^a\frac{dx}{x^p}\) converges if and only if \(p<1\).

Exercise 9.7.2. Hint. By definition, \(\lim_{s\to c^-}\int_a^sf\) and \(\lim_{t\to c^+}\int_t^bf\) both exist. Take \(s=c-\varepsilon\) and \(t=c+\varepsilon\).

Solution. Since \(\int_a^bf\) converges, the limits \[L_1=\lim_{s\to c^-}\int_a^sf\quad\text{and}\quad L_2=\lim_{t\to c^+}\int_t^bf\] exist, and \(\int_a^bf=L_1+L_2\). For \(0<\varepsilon<\min\{c-a,b-c\}\), let \(G(\varepsilon)=\int_a^{c-\varepsilon}f+\int_{c+\varepsilon}^bf\). We show \(G(\varepsilon)\to L_1+L_2\) as \(\varepsilon\to0^+\).

Let \(\eta>0\). There is a \(\delta_1>0\) such that \(\left|\int_a^sf-L_1\right|<\frac\eta2\) for all \(s\in[a,c)\) with \(c-s<\delta_1\). There is a \(\delta_2>0\) such that \(\left|\int_t^bf-L_2\right|<\frac\eta2\) for all \(t\in(c,b]\) with \(t-c<\delta_2\). Let \(\delta=\min\{\delta_1,\delta_2,c-a,b-c\}\). If \(0<\varepsilon<\delta\), take \(s=c-\varepsilon\) and \(t=c+\varepsilon\). Then \[|G(\varepsilon)-(L_1+L_2)|\le\left|\int_a^{c-\varepsilon}f-L_1\right|+\left|\int_{c+\varepsilon}^bf-L_2\right|<\frac\eta2+\frac\eta2=\eta.\] So \(\operatorname{P.V.}\int_a^bf=\lim_{\varepsilon\to0^+}G(\varepsilon)=L_1+L_2=\int_a^bf\).

Then \[\int_0^bx^2e^{-x^2}\,dx=-\frac b2e^{-b^2}+\frac12\int_0^be^{-x^2}\,dx.\] For \(b\ge1\), \(0<be^{-b^2}\le be^{-b}\), which tends to \(0\) by Theorem 9.4.13. So letting \(b\to\infty\) gives \(\int_0^\infty x^2e^{-x^2}\,dx=\frac12\cdot\frac{\sqrt\pi}2=\frac{\sqrt\pi}4\).

Exercise 9.9.2. Hint. Use the Intermediate Value Theorem to find \(x_i\) with \(\alpha(x_i)=\alpha(a)+\frac in[\alpha(b)-\alpha(a)]\).

Solution. Let \(n\in\mathbb{N}\). If \(\alpha(b)=\alpha(a)\), then \(\alpha\) is constant, since it is increasing. Then every partition with \(n\) subintervals works, since every \(\Delta\alpha_i=0\).

Suppose \(\alpha(a)<\alpha(b)\). For \(i=0,1,\dots,n\), let \[y_i=\alpha(a)+\frac in[\alpha(b)-\alpha(a)].\] Then \(\alpha(a)=y_0<y_1<\cdots<y_n=\alpha(b)\). Let \(x_0=a\) and \(x_n=b\). For \(0<i<n\), \(y_i\) is between \(\alpha(a)\) and \(\alpha(b)\). Since \(\alpha\) is continuous, the Intermediate Value Theorem (Theorem 6.3.10) gives an \(x_i\in(a,b)\) with \(\alpha(x_i)=y_i\). So \(\alpha(x_i)=y_i\) for every \(i\).

The points are in order. If \(x_i\le x_{i-1}\) for some \(i\), then \(\alpha(x_i)\le\alpha(x_{i-1})\), since \(\alpha\) is increasing. That says \(y_i\le y_{i-1}\), which is false. So \(a=x_0<x_1<\cdots<x_n=b\), and \(\mathcal{P}=\{x_0,\dots,x_n\}\) is a partition of \([a,b]\). For each \(i\), \[\Delta\alpha_i=y_i-y_{i-1}=\frac1n[\alpha(b)-\alpha(a)].\]

Exercise 9.9.3. Hint. Since \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) increase, the sup of \(\alpha\) on \([x_{i-1},x_i]\) is \(\alpha(x_i)\) and the inf of \(\beta\) is \(\beta(x_{i-1})\). Check that \(\alpha(x_i)\beta(x_i)-\alpha(x_{i-1})\beta(x_{i-1})=\alpha(x_i)\Delta\beta_i+\beta(x_{i-1})\Delta\alpha_i\), and sum over \(i\).

Exercise 9.9.4. Hint. By Example 9.9.6, \(\int_a^bf\,dI_{s_i}=f(s_i)\). Multiplying the integrator by \(c\ge0\) multiplies every Riemann–Stieltjes sum by \(c\). Then use Theorem 9.9.10 (3).

Solution. Step 1. Scaling the integrator. Let \(\beta:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}\) be increasing, let \(f\in\mathcal{R}(\beta)\), and let \(c\ge0\). We show \(f\in\mathcal{R}(c\beta)\) and \(\int_a^bf\,d(c\beta)=c\int_a^bf\,d\beta\). If \(c=0\), then \(c\beta\) is constant. Every sum for \(c\beta\) is \(0\), so the integral is \(0\). Suppose \(c>0\). For any partition \(\mathcal{P}\), the increments of \(c\beta\) are \(c\Delta\beta_i\). So \[\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{P},f,c\beta)=c\,\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{P},f,\beta)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P},f,c\beta)=c\,\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P},f,\beta).\] Let \(\varepsilon>0\). Riemann’s Criterion (Theorem 9.9.8) gives a partition \(\mathcal{P}\) with \(\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{P},f,\beta)-\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P},f,\beta)<\frac\varepsilon c\). Then the difference for \(c\beta\) is less than \(\varepsilon\). So \(f\in\mathcal{R}(c\beta)\). Also, for every \(\mathcal{P}\), \(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P},f,\beta)\le\int_a^bf\,d\beta\le\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{P},f,\beta)\). Multiplying by \(c\), the number \(c\int_a^bf\,d\beta\) lies between every lower sum and every upper sum for \(c\beta\). So it lies between the lower and upper integrals, which both equal \(\int_a^bf\,d(c\beta)\).

Step 2. Since \(a<s_i\le b\) and \(f\) is continuous, Example 9.9.6 gives \(f\in\mathcal{R}(I_{s_i})\) with \(\int_a^bf\,dI_{s_i}=f(s_i)\). Here \(I_{s_i}(x)=I(x-s_i)\). By Step 1, \(f\in\mathcal{R}(c_iI_{s_i})\) with \(\int_a^bf\,d(c_iI_{s_i})=c_if(s_i)\). Each \(c_iI_{s_i}\) is increasing, since \(c_i\ge0\).

Step 3. Let \(\alpha_k=\sum_{i=1}^kc_iI_{s_i}\). We show by induction on \(k\) that \(f\in\mathcal{R}(\alpha_k)\) and \(\int_a^bf\,d\alpha_k=\sum_{i=1}^kc_if(s_i)\). The case \(k=1\) is Step 2. If it holds for \(k\), then \(\alpha_{k+1}=\alpha_k+c_{k+1}I_{s_{k+1}}\) is a sum of two increasing functions. By Theorem 9.9.10 (3) and Step 2, \[\int_a^bf\,d\alpha_{k+1}=\int_a^bf\,d\alpha_k+\int_a^bf\,d(c_{k+1}I_{s_{k+1}})=\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}c_if(s_i).\] Since \(\alpha=\alpha_n\), this proves the theorem.

Exercise 9.9.7. Hint. First show that \(V_\alpha(x)=\int_a^x|\alpha'(t)|\,dt\), so that \(V_\alpha\in C^{(1)}[a,b]\). Then apply Theorem 9.9.17 to \(V_\alpha\) and to \(V_\alpha-\alpha\).

Solution. By Corollary 8.1.10, \(\alpha\in BV[a,b]\). So \(V_\alpha\) is defined.

Step 1. \(V_\alpha(x)=\int_a^x|\alpha'(t)|\,dt\) for \(x\in[a,b]\). Both sides are \(0\) at \(x=a\). Fix \(x\in(a,b]\). Since \(|\alpha'|\) is continuous, it is integrable on \([a,x]\). Let \(\mathcal{P}=\{x_0,\dots,x_n\}\) be a partition of \([a,x]\). By the Mean Value Theorem, \(\alpha(x_i)-\alpha(x_{i-1})=\alpha'(t_i)\Delta x_i\) for some \(t_i\in(x_{i-1},x_i)\). So \[V(\mathcal{P})=\sum_{i=1}^n|\alpha'(t_i)|\Delta x_i,\] and \(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P},|\alpha'|)\le V(\mathcal{P})\le\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{P},|\alpha'|)\). The left inequality and \(V(\mathcal{P})\le V_\alpha[a,x]\) give \(\int_a^x|\alpha'|=\underline{\int_{a}^{x}}|\alpha'|\le V_\alpha[a,x]\). For the other direction, let \(\mathcal{Q}\) be any partition of \([a,x]\). By Lemmas 8.1.7 and 9.1.5, \[V(\mathcal{P})\le V(\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{Q})\le\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{Q},|\alpha'|)\le\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{Q},|\alpha'|).\] So \(V(\mathcal{P})\le\overline{\int_{a}^{x}}|\alpha'|=\int_a^x|\alpha'|\) for every \(\mathcal{P}\), and \(V_\alpha[a,x]\le\int_a^x|\alpha'|\).

Step 2. By Step 1 and Theorem 9.3.7, \(V_\alpha\) is differentiable on \([a,b]\) with \(V_\alpha'=|\alpha'|\), which is continuous and so in \(\mathcal{R}[a,b]\). Let \(\beta=V_\alpha-\alpha\). Then \(\beta\) is differentiable with \(\beta'=|\alpha'|-\alpha'\in\mathcal{R}[a,b]\). Both \(V_\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are increasing, by Theorems 8.2.2 and 8.2.4. By Theorem 9.9.17, \(f\in\mathcal{R}(V_\alpha)\) and \(f\in\mathcal{R}(\beta)\), with \[\int_a^bf\,dV_\alpha=\int_a^bf|\alpha'|\quad\text{and}\quad\int_a^bf\,d\beta=\int_a^bf\bigl(|\alpha'|-\alpha'\bigr).\] By Definition 9.9.20, \(f\in\mathcal{R}(\alpha)\), and by Theorem 9.2.11, \[\int_a^bf\,d\alpha=\int_a^bf|\alpha'|-\int_a^bf\bigl(|\alpha'|-\alpha'\bigr)=\int_a^bf\alpha'.\]
